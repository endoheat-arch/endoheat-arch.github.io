This is general pet-care organizing help, not veterinary or medical advice. Your veterinarian is the right source for diet, medication doses, vaccine timing and anything about your pet's health. Use these steps to hand over what your vet has already told you, not to invent a care plan.

1. Start your pet sitter checklist printable the week before you leave

The best time to fill in a pet sitter checklist printable is not the night before the flight. Give yourself at least a few days so you can walk through a normal day with the checklist in hand and catch anything you forgot.

  • Print one Pet Sitter Info page per pet (two dogs means two sheets, even if the same person is sitting)
  • Fill in the basics first: your phone, a backup contact, the vet clinic and the nearest emergency clinic
  • Walk through one full day and write what actually happens, not what you wish happened
  • Put the finished sheet on the fridge or in a folder the sitter will see as soon as they walk in
  • Leave a spare copy in the same folder as vaccine certificates, in case boarding or an emergency visit needs them

If you already keep a Happy Paws log for vaccination and vet records, you do not need to rewrite everything. Copy the feeding amount, medication names and vet phone onto the sitter page once, then keep the rest of the binder for your own records.

2. What to leave for a pet sitter (the short list)

People search what to leave for pet sitter because the mental list grows fast. Here is a practical version that covers most dogs, cats and small pets:

  • Written instructions. One page the sitter can read without unlocking your phone.
  • Food. Measured portions or clearly labeled containers, plus a little extra in case the trip runs long.
  • Treats and enrichment. Whatever keeps your pet calm: a favorite chew, a puzzle feeder, a brush.
  • Medications. In original packaging when possible, with the schedule written on the checklist (name, how much, when). Do not leave dosing decisions to memory or a rushed text.
  • Leashes, harnesses, litter supplies, waste bags. Put them in one obvious spot.
  • Keys and entry notes. Door codes, alarm instructions, which lock sticks.
  • Emergency cash or card details only if you already trust this person and have agreed on that ahead of time. Otherwise keep payment simple and separate.
  • Your itinerary. Flight numbers or hotel name are optional; what matters is when you will be reachable and when you expect to be back.

If the sitter is new to your home, do a 15-minute walkthrough once with the checklist in hand. Point at the food bin, the litter closet, the spare key hook and the pet's favorite hiding spot. Paper plus a short tour beats a long text thread every time.

3. Write pet sitter instructions the sitter can follow without calling you

Pet sitter instructions work best when they are specific and short. Vague lines such as "feed normally" or "walk as needed" create follow-up questions. Aim for lines a stranger could follow on day one:

  • Feeding schedule. "Breakfast 7:30 am: 1 cup of Brand X adult formula. Dinner 6 pm: same amount. Fresh water morning and evening."
  • Walk times. "Morning walk 20–30 minutes around the block. Evening walk after dinner. Use the red leash on the hook by the door. Avoid the dog park on the south side — he gets overexcited there."
  • Medications. Copy the label: medicine name, amount, time of day, with or without food. If a pill is hidden in a treat, say which treat and where the bag lives.
  • House rules. "No couch. Crate at night with the soft blue blanket. Do not let him out the front door alone — he bolts."
  • Personality notes. "She hides under the bed when the doorbell rings. Coax with a treat; do not drag her out."

Fill every row, even if the answer is "none" or "same as usual," so the sitter does not wonder whether you forgot.

4. Fill in a pet sitter info sheet for dogs, cats and multi-pet homes

A pet sitter info sheet is the same idea as the checklist, just written as a handoff page. A few species-specific notes help:

Dogs

  • Which leash and harness, where the bags are, and whether the dog pulls or reacts to other dogs
  • Crate, bed or "free roam" preference overnight
  • Any yard gate that must stay closed

Cats

  • Litter box locations and scooping rules (once a day? twice?)
  • Whether the cat is indoor-only
  • Favorite hiding spots and how to check that the cat has eaten without forcing contact

Multi-pet homes

  • Feed separately if anyone steals food
  • Note which pets get along and which need staggered playtime
  • One sheet per pet still wins. A single shared page gets crowded and easy to misread

If you have rabbits, birds or other companions, add cage-cleaning notes, temperature preferences and which rooms are off-limits. The Happy Paws pages are undated and work for dogs, cats, rabbits and other companions; print a fresh sitter page whenever the routine changes.

5. Dog sitter instructions: walks, keys and a backup plan

Dog sitter instructions usually live or die on logistics. Beyond food and walks, spell out:

  • Keys and locks. Which key, which door, whether the deadbolt needs a second turn.
  • Neighbors. "The neighbor in 4B knows the dog and has a spare key if you get locked out. Text me before you use it."
  • Weather backup. "If it is storming, do a shorter yard potty break and play indoors with the rope toy."
  • If the dog escapes. Your phone first, then the backup contact, then the microchip registry number from the pet profile page.
  • Vet threshold. "If he vomits once and acts normal, note it. If he vomits twice, seems lethargic or will not drink, call the emergency clinic at [number] and then call me."

Those thresholds are examples of how to phrase a handoff, not veterinary rules. Ask your own vet what warning signs matter for your dog before you travel, and write their guidance on the sheet.

7. How to prepare for a pet sitter the night before

How to prepare for a pet sitter the evening before departure is mostly about reducing decision-making for them:

  • Set out tomorrow's food portions in labeled containers
  • Put medications next to the food with the schedule visible
  • Charge any camera you already use (optional; never a surprise — tell the sitter if a camera is on)
  • Take out trash that would smell by day three
  • Leave the thermostat where the pets are comfortable
  • Put the printed checklist, tracker, spare key and emergency numbers in one obvious folder labeled "Pet sitter"
  • Send the sitter a photo of that folder's location and your approximate return time

Do a final walk-through with the sheet in your hand. If you catch yourself saying "Oh, and one more thing…" write it in the Notes row instead of trusting memory.

8. Common pet sitter checklist questions

Do I need a printable if I already texted instructions? A text thread is easy to lose mid-visit. A printed pet sitter checklist printable stays on the counter. Many sitters prefer both: a text for your phone number, and paper for the routine.

Should the sitter also get vaccine records? For a house-sit, usually not unless an emergency clinic asks. Keep certificates in the same binder so they are findable. For boarding or daycare, clinics and kennels often want the official documents — that is a different handoff than a weekend sitter.

What if my pet's routine is complicated? Break it into morning and evening blocks on the sheet. Long paragraphs get skimmed; short labeled rows get followed.

Can kids in the house help? Yes, with supervision. A chore-style tick list (the weekly tracker) is easier for a helper than a wall of text. Pair it with an adult who owns the medications.
