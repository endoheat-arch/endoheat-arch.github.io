Intro: why "where" matters as much as "how much"

If you run a side hustle, sell digital products, or freelance, you probably have money that isn't for long-term investing: an emergency cushion, a tax set-aside, or cash for next month's business costs. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes an emergency fund as a cash reserve for unplanned expenses and says it should be kept somewhere safe, accessible, and not tempting to spend.

Several common places to hold short-term cash sound alike but work very differently, especially when it comes to what protects your money. This post compares five of them using official sources. It's general education, not financial advice, and it doesn't recommend any specific bank, fund or product. Rates and rules change, so check current terms before you decide.

Savings and money market accounts: insured deposits

A regular savings account and a money market deposit account (MMDA) are both bank or credit union deposit accounts.

What protects them: The FDIC lists savings accounts, money market deposit accounts and CDs among the deposits it covers, up to $250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, for each account ownership category. The FDIC also says that since it was founded in 1933, no depositor has lost a penny of FDIC-insured funds. At federally insured credit unions, share accounts get similar coverage from the NCUA, with each member having at least $250,000 in total coverage.

How money market accounts differ: According to the CFPB, money market accounts tend to pay higher interest than other savings accounts, but usually limit transactions by check, debit card or electronic transfer, and may require a minimum deposit.

The rate can move: Many deposit accounts are variable-rate accounts. Under the federal Truth in Savings rules, a bank must tell you up front that a variable rate may change, how it's determined and how often it can change, but it doesn't have to give advance notice of each rate change. The rate you sign up for isn't locked in.

Money market funds: an investment, not a deposit

This is where the similar names cause the most confusion. A money market fund is a type of mutual fund that invests in liquid, short-term debt securities, cash and cash equivalents. The CFPB is clear that a money market mutual fund account is considered an investment, not a savings or checking account, even if it allows check writing.

The $1.00 share price: Most retail and government money market funds try to keep a stable value of $1.00 per share. That isn't guaranteed. If the value per share moves more than half a cent away from $1.00, the fund has to reprice, which is called "breaking the buck". The SEC says this happens very rarely, but if it does, your holding loses value.

What protects them: Money market funds are not guaranteed by the FDIC like bank accounts are, and that's true even when a bank sells the fund and it carries the bank's name. If they're held at a SIPC-member brokerage, SIPC treats money market funds as securities and protects against a failed firm's missing assets, up to $500,000 including a $250,000 limit for cash. SIPC is explicit that it does not protect against a decline in the value of your securities, so it isn't the same as FDIC insurance.

Costs and inflation: The SEC notes that money market fund returns have historically been lower than other fund types, that inflation can outpace those returns, and that when short-term rates are very low, fund fees can exceed the fund's income.

CDs: a fixed term in exchange for a stated rate

A certificate of deposit holds a fixed amount of money for a fixed period, such as six months, one year or five years, and the bank pays interest in return.

What protects them: Bank CDs are FDIC-insured and credit union CDs are NCUA-insured, each up to $250,000. Investor.gov points out that the limit covers all your accounts in your name at the same bank, not each CD separately.

The trade-off is access. Taking money out early means paying a penalty. The OCC, which regulates national banks, says banks are generally required by law to charge an early withdrawal penalty, with some exceptions. The CFPB suggests comparing the term, the interest rate and the size of the early withdrawal penalty, and picking a maturity date that matches when you expect to need the money.

Brokered CDs: Some CDs are sold through brokers. Investor.gov warns that deposit brokers aren't licensed or certified by any state or federal agency, so check the issuer and the seller before buying.

Treasury bills: short-term loans to the U.S. government

Treasury bills are sold by the U.S. Treasury in terms of 4, 6, 8, 13, 17, 26 and 52 weeks. You buy at a discount or at face value and receive the face value at maturity; the difference is your interest. The minimum purchase on TreasuryDirect is $100, in $100 increments, and you can hold a bill until it matures or sell it earlier.

Taxes: Interest on T-bills is subject to federal tax but not state or local taxes.

What protects them: T-bills aren't bank deposits, and the FDIC lists Treasury bills, bonds and notes as not covered by deposit insurance. They're debt issued by the U.S. government itself.

Side-by-side comparison

  • Savings account: bank or credit union deposit; FDIC or NCUA insured up to the limits; rate usually variable.
  • Money market account: bank or credit union deposit; FDIC or NCUA insured; often a higher rate but transaction limits and possible minimums.
  • Money market fund: mutual fund investment; not FDIC insured; aims for $1.00 per share but can lose value.
  • CD: bank or credit union deposit; FDIC or NCUA insured; fixed term with an early withdrawal penalty.
  • Treasury bill: U.S. government debt; 4 to 52 week terms; federal tax only on interest.

Questions to ask before you choose

  • When might I need this money? Match the term to your timeline, as the CFPB suggests for CDs.
  • What exactly is protecting it? Look for the FDIC or NCUA name for deposits, and confirm your bank with the FDIC's BankFind tool or your credit union with the NCUA's lookup.
  • Is the rate fixed or variable? Read the account disclosure.
  • What are the fees and penalties? For funds, read the prospectus; for CDs, the early withdrawal terms.
  • Am I near the insurance limit? The FDIC's Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator can help you check.

Conclusion

"Safe place for cash" can mean several different things. Savings accounts, money market accounts and CDs are insured deposits, up to the limits. Money market funds are investments that aim for a steady $1.00 share price but aren't FDIC insured. Treasury bills are short-term government debt with their own tax treatment. Know which one you're using, what protects it, and what it costs to get your money out early, then check current rates and terms yourself or with a qualified professional before you decide.
