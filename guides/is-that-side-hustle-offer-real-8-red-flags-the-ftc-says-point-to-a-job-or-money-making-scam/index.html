Intro: why side-income seekers are a target

If you're looking for extra income, you've probably seen offers that sound almost too easy: rate a few products, "boost" some apps, or follow a coach's system and watch the money roll in. Scammers know many people want flexible work. The Federal Trade Commission warns that they send emails, texts and job ads claiming you can make thousands of dollars a month from home with little time and effort, when what they really want is your money or your personal information (FTC, How to avoid work-from-home job scams).

This post turns the FTC's consumer guidance into a practical red-flag checklist you can run through before you accept any job or money-making offer. It's general education, not legal or financial advice.

What the FTC's numbers show

This isn't a niche problem. The FTC says reported job scams grew to more than 105,000 in 2024, nearly three times the 2020 level, with reported losses of over $513 million (FTC Business Blog, September 2025). Across all fraud types, consumers reported $15.9 billion in losses in 2025, up from over $12 billion the year before (FTC, March 2026).

The FTC also notes that most frauds are never reported, so its figures likely reflect only a fraction of the actual harm (FTC Data Spotlight, December 2024).

Red flag 1: an unexpected job text or chat message

A message out of the blue on WhatsApp, Telegram, text or social media offering easy online work is a classic opener. The FTC's advice is blunt: ignore generic and unexpected messages about jobs, because real employers will never contact you that way (FTC, How to spot and avoid task scams).

A newer twist: fake recruiters who claim to work for companies you know may ask you to reply "YES" or "INTERESTED" instead of clicking a link. The FTC says not to reply, however professional the message looks (FTC, That job offer text is probably a scam).

Red flag 2: you have to pay to get paid

This is the one rule worth memorizing. The FTC says honest employers, including the federal government, will never ask you to pay to get a job (FTC, Job Scams). Instead of earning money, people end up paying for starter kits, so-called training or certifications that turn out to be useless (FTC, Job Scams).

The same goes for placement services. The FTC notes that honest placement firms don't usually charge job candidates, because the hiring company pays them. If a firm wants a fee, especially upfront, walk away (FTC, Job Scams).

Red flag 3: "work" that feels like a game

"Task scams," also called gamified job scams, ask you to complete simple, repetitive tasks such as liking videos or rating product images in an app, supposedly for a commission on each click. According to the FTC, there are no real commissions (FTC, Task scams create the illusion of making money).

The pattern is predictable. The app shows a growing balance of fake earnings, and scammers may pay out a little, usually $5 to $20, to build trust. Then they ask you to deposit your own money, usually in crypto, to unlock more tasks or withdraw your "earnings." That money is gone (FTC, How to spot and avoid task scams).

The FTC estimated that reports of task scams went from about 5,000 in all of 2023 to about 20,000 in just the first half of 2024 (FTC Data Spotlight, December 2024). Also be wary of anyone offering to pay you for positive ratings or likes; the FTC says no honest company will do that (FTC, How to spot and avoid task scams).

Red flag 4: a check you're asked to deposit and partly send back

In a fake check scam, a stranger or new "employer" sends a check, often for more than they owe you, and asks you to send part of it back or pass it on as gift cards, crypto or a wire transfer. Fake checks can look real even to bank employees, and it can take weeks for a bank to discover the fraud. By then, you're on the hook for the money (FTC, Fake Check Scams).

This shows up in nanny, caregiver, virtual assistant and mystery-shopper "jobs." The FTC's rule of thumb: if an offer involves depositing a check and then using some of the money for any reason, it's a scam (FTC, Job Scams).

Red flag 5: receiving and reshipping packages

Titles like "quality control manager" or "delivery operations specialist" can hide a reshipping scam. You're asked to receive packages, throw away the original box and receipt, and send the items to another address, often overseas. The goods are typically bought with stolen card or bank information, the promised paycheck never arrives, and the payroll details you handed over can lead to identity theft (FTC, Can you unbox the signs of a reshipping scam?). The FTC is clear: reshipping goods is never a real job (FTC, Job Scams).

Red flag 6: guaranteed income or a "proven system"

Not every scam is pitched as a job. Some are business coaching programs or "done-for-you" online business offers. The FTC says that if a program promises guaranteed income, large returns or a "proven system," it's likely a scam, and even a free or low-cost starter system can turn into a money pit of expensive mentoring and upsells (FTC, When a Business Offer or Coaching Program Is a Scam).

The FTC also points out that there's no licensing requirement to become a business coach, and scammers often lie about credentials, so research any certification a coach claims and talk to former students (FTC, When a Business Offer or Coaching Program Is a Scam).

Red flag 7: pressure to decide now

Scammers push you to get in now or "risk losing out." The FTC recommends taking your time, asking questions and getting a second opinion from someone who has your best interests in mind (FTC, How to spot and avoid business coaching scams). Talking it over also buys you time to think (FTC, Job Scams).

Red flag 8: glowing testimonials you can't verify

Success stories are easy to fake. The FTC advises reading testimonials with skepticism, since glowing stories could be fake or misleading and positive reviews may come from made-up profiles (FTC, How to spot and avoid business coaching scams).

A quick checklist before you say yes

Based on the FTC's guidance, run through these before accepting any offer:

  • Did this reach me through an unexpected text, chat app or social media message?
  • Am I being asked to pay anything to start, unlock work or withdraw earnings?
  • Is the "work" mostly liking, rating or clicking in an app?
  • Is anyone sending me a check and asking for money back?
  • Does the job involve receiving and forwarding packages?
  • Does anyone promise guaranteed income or a "proven system"?
  • Am I being rushed?
  • Have I searched the company or person's name plus "scam," "review" or "complaint" (FTC, Job Scams)?
  • Have I checked with my state attorney general for complaints (FTC, When a Business Offer or Coaching Program Is a Scam)?

A "yes" to any of the first seven questions is a reason to stop. And note the FTC's caveat: finding no complaints doesn't guarantee a company is honest (FTC, Job Scams).

If you already paid or shared information

The FTC says to contact the company you used to send money right away, whether that was a card, payment app, wire transfer, gift card or crypto, report the fraud, and ask to have the transaction reversed if possible (FTC, Job Scams). If you shared personal details such as a bank account or Social Security number, IdentityTheft.gov has steps to protect yourself (FTC, reshipping scam alert). Report scams to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and you can also tell your state attorney general (FTC, Job Scams).

Conclusion

Real side income usually builds slowly through actual work: freelancing, selling products people want, or offering a service. If an offer asks you to pay first, promises guaranteed money, or arrives as a surprise text, treat it as a scam until proven otherwise.
