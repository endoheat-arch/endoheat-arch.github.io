How to Price Digital Printables and Planners: Cost Floor, Value Ceiling and Competitor Checks

Originally published on Telegraph

Outline

  • Intro: three checks beat one lucky number
  • Build a cost floor (time, tools, fees)
  • Estimate a value ceiling
  • Competitor and market check
  • Psychological pricing (charm pricing, tiers)
  • Payhip options and FTC sale rules
  • Checklist and conclusion

Pricing a printable or planner is less about a lucky number and more about three checks: what it costs you to create and sell, what it is worth to the buyer, and what similar products already charge. Stripe describes that hybrid clearly: use cost-based pricing for your floor and value-based pricing for your ceiling, then choose a price in between.

This is a practical how-to—not financial, legal or tax advice, and not a promise that any price will sell. Fees change, so verify the linked sources before you lock anything in. I sell planners on Payhip, including the Calm Money Monthly Planner; examples below are for illustration only.

Build a cost floor

Stripe’s cost-based overview starts with total cost per unit, then a markup. For a digital printable, “per unit” is mostly your time and tools, plus what you pay on every sale.

Creation costs can include design software, licensed fonts or stock, and hours spent on templates, covers and mockups. Spread those across a realistic sales count—not a fantasy one.

Per-sale costs depend on the platform. Payhip’s pricing page lists Free Forever ($0/month + 5%), Plus ($29/month + 2%), and Pro ($99/month with no Payhip transaction fee). Payhip states that PayPal and Stripe still charge at their standard rates on every plan. Stripe’s published US rate for domestic cards is 2.9% + 30¢ per successful transaction.

Illustrative $12 sale on Payhip Free with Stripe (domestic card): Payhip’s 5% is $0.60; Stripe at 2.9% + 30¢ is about $0.65; total ≈ $1.25 before other taxes or refunds. Your net is not $12. Price above that floor or you subsidize every download.

The U.S. Small Business Administration also ties pricing to break-even: Fixed Costs ÷ (Price − Variable Costs) = Break-Even Point in Units. For a digital seller, fixed costs might be software and a paid Payhip plan; variable costs are mainly transaction and processing fees. Break-even will not tell you what buyers will pay—but it stops unpaid work.

Estimate a value ceiling

Value-based pricing starts with buyer outcomes—time saved, confusion avoided, a habit supported—not how long design took. Identify the value, quantify it roughly (for example, “replaces a paper planner pad” or “saves rebuilding the same weekly grid”), set a price between your cost floor and that ceiling, and spell the value out on the product page.

A one-page chore chart and a multi-section wedding binder should not share one arbitrary tag by default. More pages, clearer instructions, fillable vs print-only files, and niche specificity usually support a higher ceiling—if the listing makes that difference obvious. Stripe’s point still holds: leave the buyer feeling they come out ahead.

Run a competitor and market check

The SBA’s market research guide asks: Pricing: What do potential customers pay for these alternatives? and Market saturation: How many similar options are already available?

A practical competitor pass for printables:

  • Search for products in the same niche and format (for example, “weekly meal planner printable PDF”).
  • Note listed prices, page counts, fillable vs print-only, and what mockups promise.
  • Separate marketplaces (discovery + listing rules) from direct storefronts like Payhip (your own traffic).
  • Record a range—do not invent a market average you did not measure.

Competitive prices are a reality check, not a mandate. Stripe warns that pure cost-plus ignores demand and competition; the SBA frames competitive analysis as learning who else serves your buyer so you can define an edge. If your file is thinner than the lower-priced options and priced like a premium bundle, rewrite the offer or the price.

Use psychological pricing carefully

Harvard Business School’s Elie Ofek, in HBS Working Knowledge, covers tactics common in digital shops:

  • Charm pricing (.99 endings) leans on left-digit bias—$9.99 can feel closer to $9 than to $10 for some buyers. Ofek notes it is so common it may no longer give a big edge, but skipping it can look atypical.
  • Tiered pricing (basic / standard / premium) can steer buyers when differences are clear—useful for a single printable, a small pack, and a full binder.
  • Anchor + discount (was $X, now $Y) only works when the “was” price is real (see below).

These are presentation choices after the economics make sense.

Payhip options—and FTC rules if you run a sale

On Payhip you usually set a fixed price when you add a digital product. You can also use Pay What You Want by typing `+` after the amount: `5+` means a $5 minimum with room to pay more; `0+` allows any amount including free, per Payhip’s PWYW help. Payhip notes Stripe does not allow donation-only flows without something tangible in return. PWYW can teach you what buyers pay above a floor; it does not erase fee math.

If you later advertise “was $18, now $12,” the FTC’s Guides Against Deceptive Pricing (16 CFR Part 233) apply. A former price used for comparison should be a bona fide price offered to the public on a regular basis for a reasonably substantial period in the recent course of business—not an inflated number invented for the discount. “Formerly sold” language generally requires substantial sales at that price. Meaningless cuts (for example, framing $10 → $9.99 as a big bargain) can also mislead. Earn the sale story with a real prior asking price, or simply set the price you mean to charge.

A simple pricing checklist

1. Compute a cost floor including Payhip plan fees and Stripe/PayPal processing (Payhip; Stripe). 2. Write one sentence on buyer value; set a ceiling below that story (Stripe value-based guide). 3. Sample competitor prices in the same niche (SBA). 4. Pick a number between floor and ceiling; decide on .99 or a round number (HBS charm pricing). 5. On Payhip, choose fixed price or PWYW (PWYW help); consider a clear tier or bundle. 6. Before “was/now,” confirm the former price meets FTC Part 233. 7. Revisit with real sales data—not after one quiet week.

A monthly money sheet such as the Calm Money Monthly Planner can track list price, fees and net so pricing stays tied to numbers you can see.

Conclusion

Price printables with a cost floor (including platform and processing fees), a value ceiling (what the buyer gets), and a competitor check. Layer charm endings, tiers and honest sales only after those three are solid, and keep FTC former-price rules in mind when you advertise a discount. There is no universal “correct” printable price—only a defensible range you can explain and revise with evidence.
