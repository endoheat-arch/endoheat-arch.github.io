What a Roth IRA is (and is not)

A Roth IRA is a personal retirement account you open with a bank, brokerage or other provider. Per the IRS Roth IRA overview, you generally cannot deduct contributions, qualified distributions can be tax-free if you meet the rules, you can keep contributing after age 70½ (there is no age cutoff for regular contributions under current law for years 2020 and later), and you can leave money in the account for life. The account must be designated as a Roth IRA when it is set up.

Investor.gov describes the trade-off in one sentence: contributions are made with after-tax dollars; earnings and qualified withdrawals are generally tax-free. A traditional IRA is the reverse pattern for many people — contributions may be deductible, and withdrawals are usually taxed as income.

This post is general education drawn from IRS and Investor.gov materials. It is not tax, legal or personalized financial advice, and it does not promise any income, return or tax outcome. Rules depend on your filing status, income and other plans. Confirm details in the official sources linked below or with a qualified professional.

Who can contribute: taxable compensation and MAGI

IRS Topic No. 309 says you can contribute to a Roth IRA if you have taxable compensation and your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) is within the year’s limits. Contributions are not deductible. Qualified distributions are not included in income when the rules are met.

Compensation. Publication 590-A defines compensation as what you earn from working. It includes wages, salaries, tips, professional fees, bonuses and similar pay for personal services. For self-employed people (sole proprietors or partners), compensation is net earnings from the trade or business when your personal services are a material income-producing factor, reduced by retirement-plan contributions made on your behalf and the deductible portion of self-employment tax. Interest, dividends, pension income and earnings from property generally do not count as compensation for IRA purposes.

Spousal IRAs. If you file a joint return, you may be able to contribute for a spouse who has little or no taxable compensation, as long as the couple’s combined taxable compensation covers both contributions. The IRS explains this under spousal IRAs and Publication 590-A.

Income phase-outs (Roth-specific). Even if you have compensation, Roth contributions can be reduced or eliminated when MAGI is high. Per the IRS IR-2025-111 announcement and Publication 590-A:

  • 2025: Phase-out for singles and heads of household is between $150,000 and $165,000; for married filing jointly (and qualifying surviving spouses) between $236,000 and $246,000.
  • 2026: Phase-out for singles and heads of household is between $153,000 and $168,000; for married filing jointly between $242,000 and $252,000.
  • Married filing separately (and living with your spouse at any time during the year) remains a narrow $0–$10,000 phase-out range.

Above the top of the range, you generally cannot make a regular Roth contribution for that year. Topic No. 309 notes that conversions from a traditional IRA (or certain plan rollovers) to a Roth IRA are a separate topic and are not limited the same way by MAGI for the conversion itself — but conversions have their own tax consequences. This post does not walk through conversion strategies.

2025 and 2026 contribution dollar limits

The IRS IRA contribution limits page sets a combined ceiling for all traditional and Roth IRAs you own:

  • 2025: $7,000, or $8,000 if you are age 50 or older — or, if less, your taxable compensation for the year.
  • 2026: $7,500, or $8,600 if you are age 50 or older — or, if less, your taxable compensation for the year.

IR-2025-111 confirms the 2026 IRA limit increase to $7,500 and the age-50 catch-up increase to $1,100 (bringing the age-50+ total to $8,600). Rollover contributions do not count against that annual limit.

You can contribute to an IRA even if you also participate in a workplace or business retirement plan. For a Roth IRA, the main income gate is the MAGI phase-out above — not whether you have a 401(k) at a day job. Traditional IRA deductions can be limited when you (or your spouse) are covered by a workplace plan; that is a different rule set on the same IRS page.

Deadline. Contributions for a tax year are generally due by the due date of your individual income tax return for that year (not counting extensions in the usual IRA contribution deadline rules described in Publication 590-A). Confirm the exact calendar date for the year you are funding with your tax calendar and custodian.

Excess contributions. Putting in more than the limit (or contributing when you are not eligible) can trigger a 6% tax each year the excess remains in the IRA, unless you remove it (and related earnings) by the return due date including extensions, under the conditions in Publication 590-A.

Side-hustle angle: self-employment as compensation

Freelance invoices, digital-product profits and other Schedule C-style activity can create the compensation needed for an IRA contribution — but only to the extent of net self-employment earnings after the Publication 590-A adjustments. A loss from self-employment is not subtracted from wages when figuring total compensation for IRA purposes, per Publication 590-A; wages and self-employment net earnings are still computed under those rules.

Practical takeaways for side-hustle earners:

  • Track business income and expenses carefully so you know net earnings before you pledge a contribution.
  • Your Roth (and traditional) IRA contribution cannot exceed taxable compensation for the year, even if the dollar limit is higher.
  • A Roth IRA is separate from employer-style plans such as a SEP-IRA or one-participant 401(k). Those plans can allow much larger employer contributions based on business earnings; a Roth IRA’s personal contribution ceiling is the much smaller IRA limit above. For a comparison of SEP vs Solo 401(k) structures, see Geo Ace’s earlier post on that topic in this series.

Withdrawals in plain English

IRS Topic No. 451 summarizes the Roth pattern: return of your contributions is not subject to tax; earnings on qualified distributions (for example, after age 59½ and after the five-year period that begins with the first tax year for which a contribution was made to a Roth IRA) are also not taxed. Earnings on nonqualified distributions can be taxable.

Federal regulations (26 CFR § 1.408A-6) define a qualified distribution as one made after a five-taxable-year period and that is made on or after age 59½, or because of death, disability, or a qualifying first-time home purchase exception. The five-year clock generally starts on the first day of the tax year of your first regular Roth contribution (or earlier conversion year, if that came first).

Topic No. 557 explains the additional 10% tax that can apply to early distributions that are included in income, with listed exceptions. Order-of-withdrawal and basis rules are detailed in Publication 590-B; use those official materials before assuming a withdrawal is penalty-free.

How a Roth IRA sits next to a day-job plan or Solo 401(k)/SEP

  • Day-job 401(k): You can still fund a Roth IRA if compensation and MAGI allow. Elective deferrals into the 401(k) do not reduce the IRA dollar limit, but high W-2 income can push you into the Roth MAGI phase-out.
  • SEP or Solo 401(k): Those are business retirement plans with their own contribution formulas and (often) much higher potential dollar amounts than an IRA. Funding them does not by itself replace or cancel the IRA limit, but SEP employer contributions reduce the self-employment compensation figure used for IRA purposes under Publication 590-A.
  • Investments inside the account: Investor.gov notes you choose a provider and typically invest in mutual funds or similar options the provider offers. The SEC does not regulate IRAs as a product category the way it oversees securities markets; tax rules live with the IRS.

A simple checklist before you open one

1. Confirm you will have (or already have) taxable compensation for the contribution year. 2. Estimate MAGI against the year’s Roth phase-out table from the IRS newsroom or Publication 590-A. 3. Cap the contribution at the lesser of the annual IRA limit (with age-50 catch-up if applicable) and your taxable compensation. 4. Decide whether a Roth fits your preference for after-tax contributions and possible tax-free qualified withdrawals — versus a traditional IRA’s possible upfront deduction. 5. Open the account with a reputable custodian, designate it as a Roth IRA, and keep contribution records. 6. If you also use a SEP or Solo 401(k), recalculate self-employment compensation after plan contributions before finalizing the IRA amount. 7. Re-check eligibility before the tax-filing deadline if income changed during the year.

Conclusion

A Roth IRA is one of the simpler retirement tools available to people with taxable compensation — including side-hustle net earnings — as long as MAGI stays inside the IRS phase-out ranges. The 2025 and 2026 dollar limits are published, the compensation definition is in Publication 590-A, and withdrawal tax treatment turns on the five-year rule and age or other qualifying events. Use the official links below, keep records, and treat this as a map of the rules — not a recommendation to contribute any specific amount.
