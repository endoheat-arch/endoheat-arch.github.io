How to Track Rent and Repairs When You Only Own a Few Units

Originally published on Telegraph

You inherited a duplex, bought a condo as a sideline, or somehow ended up with a handful of rentals and no property manager. Congrats — and also, where did that text about the leaky faucet go? Small landlords lose time (and sometimes money) not because the work is hard, but because rent dates, repair receipts, and lease details live in five different places.

Print one set per property or per year and fill it in as you go.

Below is a practical system you can run with a notebook, a spreadsheet, or that printable. Use what fits; the point is one place for the truth.

Why a few units still need a system

One unit can hide in your head. Three units cannot. Late rent, a deposit dispute, or a tax-prep scramble all get worse when you reconstruct history from bank apps and group texts. You do not need enterprise software. You need a repeatable habit: write it down the day it happens, in the same place every time.

This is organizing for accidental and small landlords (roughly 1–10 units). It is not legal, tax, or financial advice. Local landlord-tenant rules vary; when something is unclear, check your lease and a qualified professional where you live.

1. Start with a one-page property snapshot

For each building or address, write the basics on a single sheet: street address, purchase or inheritance date if you track it, number of units, mortgage or insurance contacts, utility account numbers you pay, and where keys and spare locks live. Add the property manager or handyman number if you have one.

When a vendor asks for the address, gate code, or meter location, you will not dig through old emails. Keep a photo of the page on your phone and the paper in a binder labeled by address.

2. Build a unit roster before you need it

List every unit: unit ID, bedrooms/baths, current rent, lease end date, and occupancy status (occupied / notice / vacant). Update it when someone gives notice or rents change. A roster turns "which unit is vacant again?" into a ten-second glance.

If you only have one or two units, still fill the roster. Scale is not the point; a consistent format is. When you add a third unit later, the same sheet still works.

3. Log rent the day it hits (or the day it doesn't)

Keep a monthly rent roll: unit, amount due, date received, method (check, ACH, cash app — whatever you use), and notes for partial payments or late fees you actually assessed. Mark empty cells for unpaid rent the same week, not at month-end when memory fades.

Late rent conversations go better when you can say "I show $X due on the 1st; I have not received it as of the 8th" instead of scrolling for a Venmo screenshot. Whatever you collect, match it to a written line. If you waive a late fee once, note that too so you remember the exception.

4. Track leases and deposits like you will need them later

On one sheet per unit (or one table for all units), record lease start and end, deposit amount held, where the deposit money sits, and a short return checklist for move-out: walk-through done, keys returned, forwarding address, itemized deductions if any, and date returned or disputed.

Deposit disputes are where missing paperwork hurts most. You do not need a courtroom. You need dates and a clear list of what was charged and why, backed by your move-out inspection notes. Follow your state's rules for timelines and notices; the tracker is for your records, not a substitute for complying with local law.

5. Keep tenant contact cards current

Name, preferred phone, email, emergency contact, and vehicle or parking notes if you care about them. Update when someone changes jobs or numbers. When a pipe bursts at 10 p.m., you want one card, not a search through old leases in a drawer.

Store sensitive info carefully. A paper binder at home is fine for many small landlords; do not leave it in a common hallway or post private numbers in shared drives without thought.

6. Run a maintenance and repair log

Every request gets a line: date reported, unit, issue, who fixed it (you, handyman, plumber), cost, and date closed. Add a note if it was emergency vs. routine. Photos of before/after help if a tenant later claims the same issue was never fixed.

Patterns show up on paper: the same faucet three times may mean replace, not patch. The log also feeds your expense tracker and gives a clean history if you sell the property or hand it to a manager later.

7. Separate rental expenses from your personal budget

Keep a simple expense list for the rentals: date, category (repairs, supplies, insurance, utilities you pay, professional fees), amount, unit if known, and whether you think it may be deductible. Hand that list to your tax preparer; do not invent tax treatment yourself.

If you also want your household budget on paper, the Calm Money Monthly Planner ($5) covers personal monthly budgeting separately from rental records. For broader small-business style sheets, Simple Bookkeeping Sheets can sit beside the rental tracker when you want income and expense pages in one place. Keep rental and personal money visually separate so you never confuse a grocery run with a water heater part.

8. Use a vacancy and turnover checklist

When a unit turns, run the same list every time: notice received, advertising or listing steps you use, showing notes, application and screening steps you actually follow, lease signed, deposit collected, keys handed over. A checklist prevents "I thought you ordered the lock change" moments between you and a co-owner or helper.

Pair it with a move-in / move-out inspection sheet: rooms, condition notes, and signatures or initials if you use them. Fill move-in with the tenant present when you can. That sheet is what you compare at move-out.

9. Put renewals and big dates on a year calendar

Mark lease ends, insurance renewals, HVAC filter months, and local registration or inspection deadlines if your city has them. Glance at the calendar monthly. Surprise lease expirations create vacant months; surprise insurance lapses create worse problems.

You do not need fancy software for this. Twelve months on one page plus a phone reminder for the next 60 days of lease ends is enough for most small portfolios.

10. Close the loop every month

Pick a short monthly ritual (same weekend each month):

  • Update the rent roll for the month just ended
  • Close or chase open repair tickets
  • Note any lease ending in the next 60 days
  • File new receipts into the expense list
  • Skim the roster for vacancies or upcoming move-outs

Thirty focused minutes beats a chaotic Saturday before taxes or a showing.

A copy-paste starter checklist

  • Property snapshot filled for every address
  • Unit roster current (rent, lease end, status)
  • This month's rent roll started; unpaid units marked
  • Lease/deposit lines complete for occupied units
  • Tenant contacts updated
  • Open repairs logged with dates and costs
  • Expenses listed separately from personal spending
  • Vacancy checklist ready for the next turn
  • Year calendar has lease ends and renewals
  • Monthly 30-minute review on the calendar

If you only finish the rent roll and the repair log this week, you are already ahead of "I'll remember."
