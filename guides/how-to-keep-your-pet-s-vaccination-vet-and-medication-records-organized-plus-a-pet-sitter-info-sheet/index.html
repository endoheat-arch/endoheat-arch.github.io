How to Keep Your Pet's Vaccination, Vet and Medication Records Organized (Plus a Pet Sitter Info Sheet)

Originally published on Telegraph

The vet's front desk asks when your dog's last rabies shot was. The boarding kennel wants proof of vaccines by Friday. Your neighbor is feeding the cat this weekend and texts, "How much food again?" If the answers live in five different places (an email from the clinic, a sticky note, a receipt in the glove box, your memory), every one of those moments turns into a scramble.

Below is a simple system for keeping your pet's records organized so you can answer those questions in seconds, whether you have one senior cat or a house full of dogs, rabbits and birds.

This guide is general organizing help only. It is not veterinary or medical advice. Your veterinarian is the right source for vaccine schedules, medication doses, diet and anything about your pet's health. Use these steps to keep track of what your vet tells you, not to replace it.

1. Start with a one-page pet profile

Before you track anything else, write down the basics that never change much. This page is what you grab in an emergency or hand to a new vet, groomer or sitter.

  • Pet's name, species, breed or mix, color and markings
  • Birthday or approximate age, and adoption date
  • Microchip number and the registry it is listed with
  • Current food brand, formula and amount per meal
  • Known allergies or sensitivities your vet has noted
  • Your vet clinic's name, phone and address, plus the nearest emergency or after-hours clinic
  • A recent photo, printed or taped on (helpful if your pet ever goes missing)

Two minutes now saves a lot of digging later. Check the microchip registry while you are at it and make sure your phone number there is current. Many people move or change numbers and forget the chip is still pointing to the old one.

2. Gather every record you already have

Pet records tend to be scattered. Spend one sitting pulling them together:

  • Clinic portal or emails: many clinics send vaccine certificates and visit summaries by email or through an online portal. Search your inbox for the clinic name.
  • Paper certificates and receipts: check folders, drawers and the car.
  • Adoption or breeder paperwork: shelters and rescues often list vaccines given before adoption.
  • Previous clinics: if you have moved, the old clinic can usually send records on request.
  • Pet license tags or city paperwork: useful for confirming rabies vaccine dates in many areas.

You do not need everything perfect on day one. Write down what you can confirm and leave a blank where you are unsure. At the next appointment, ask the clinic to print or email a full history so you can fill in the gaps.

3. Keep a vaccination table with "given" and "due next" columns

The question you get asked most is "Is your pet up to date?" A table answers it at a glance. For each vaccine your vet has given, record:

  • Vaccine name, exactly as written on the certificate
  • Date given
  • Clinic or vet who gave it
  • Date the vet says the next one is due

The "due next" column is the important one. Copy it from the certificate or ask the clinic; schedules differ by pet, vaccine type, age and where you live, so do not guess. Then put the due dates on your phone calendar with a reminder a couple of weeks ahead, so you have time to book an appointment before a trip or boarding stay.

Keep the original certificates in a folder or sheet protector behind your log. Kennels, groomers and daycares often want to see the official document, not a handwritten note.

4. Track medications and preventatives in one place

Flea, tick and heartworm preventatives, allergy pills, ear drops, a course of antibiotics after a procedure: it is easy to lose track of what was given and when, especially if more than one person in the house is helping. A medication table keeps everyone on the same page.

  • Medication name and strength, as written on the label
  • Dose and how often, exactly as your vet prescribed
  • Start date and end date (or "ongoing")
  • Who prescribed it and why, in your own words
  • Refill date or how many doses are left

Never adjust a dose or stop a medication based on a log; call your vet if something seems off. The log's job is to help you remember and report accurately. If your pet ever has a reaction, a clear record of what was given and when is exactly what the vet will want to know.

5. Log every vet visit in a sentence or two

After each appointment, while it is fresh, jot down:

  • Date and reason for the visit
  • Weight recorded at the visit
  • What the vet found or said, in plain words
  • Any instructions: new meds, diet changes, follow-up dates
  • Cost, if you are tracking pet spending

This takes about a minute and pays off later. When you notice something new months from now, you can look back and tell the vet when it first came up and what was tried before.

6. Use a weekly care tracker if more than one person helps

In households where kids, partners or roommates share pet duties, the classic problem is a dog fed twice or not at all. A simple weekly grid with a box for each day solves it:

  • Breakfast and dinner
  • Fresh water
  • Walks or play time
  • Medication or preventative doses
  • Litter box, cage or yard cleanup

Hang it on the fridge or near the food bin. Whoever does the task checks the box. No more "Did you feed her?" texts. If you have kids, this pairs nicely with a family chore chart, so feeding the pet becomes one of their regular helper jobs.

7. Watch weight and grooming over time

Small changes are easy to miss when you see your pet every day. A weight chart with a reading every month or two (at home or at the clinic) makes trends visible. Grooming notes help too: when nails were last trimmed, any lumps, skin spots or matted fur you noticed, when ears were cleaned. If something looks different, you will have dates to share with your vet instead of "I think it started a while ago."

8. Keep a supply list so you do not run out

Write down the exact food formula, litter brand, preventative product, treats your pet tolerates, and the size of each. When you are at the store, or someone else is shopping for you, there is no guessing. Noting roughly when each item runs out also helps you reorder before the last scoop.

9. Write a pet sitter info sheet before you travel

Whether a friend is popping in or a paid sitter is staying over, one page of clear instructions makes their job easier and keeps your pet's routine steady. Include:

  • Your contact info, plus a backup local contact
  • Vet and emergency clinic numbers and addresses
  • Feeding times, amounts and where food is kept
  • Medications with times and doses, exactly as prescribed
  • Walk routine, leash and harness location, and any dogs or places to avoid
  • Hiding spots, escape risks and quirks ("she hides under the guest bed during storms")
  • House rules: furniture, crate, doors to keep closed
  • What to do if something seems wrong, and what spending you have pre-approved for an emergency visit

Leave the sheet on the counter with a copy of the vaccine certificates. Many owners also let their vet clinic know in advance that a sitter may bring the pet in while they are away.

10. Keep a backup copy

Paper is great for the fridge and the sitter, but papers get lost. Snap phone photos of the profile page, vaccine table and certificates, and keep them in an album or note you can find fast. If you have several pets, keep one set of pages per pet in its own folder or a single binder with dividers.
