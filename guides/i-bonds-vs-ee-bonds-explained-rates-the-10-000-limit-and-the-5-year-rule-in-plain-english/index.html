I Bonds vs EE Bonds Explained: Rates, the $10,000 Limit and the 5-Year Rule in Plain English

Originally published on Telegraph

  • How I bond rates work: fixed rate plus inflation rate, reset every 6 months
  • How EE bonds work: a fixed rate and a 20-year doubling guarantee
  • The rules that trip people up: 12-month lock, 5-year rule, purchase limits
  • How savings bond interest is taxed (and when you report it)
  • Side-by-side comparison and questions to ask before you buy
  • Sources

Side-hustle money often arrives in lumps: a good month of sales, a big client invoice, a tax refund. Once your emergency cash is sorted, you may hear about U.S. savings bonds as a low-risk place for money you will not need for a while. This explainer walks through the two kinds the Treasury sells today, Series I and Series EE, using only the official rules from TreasuryDirect. It is general education, not personal financial, investment or tax advice, and rates change, so always check the current figures before buying.

What savings bonds are and where you buy them

The U.S. Treasury currently sells two types of savings bonds, Series EE and Series I, and you can buy them for yourself, for a child, or as a gift (TreasuryDirect: Buying savings bonds). New bonds are electronic: as of January 1, 2025, I bonds are only available electronically through a TreasuryDirect account (TreasuryDirect: I bonds), and new EE bonds are electronic only as well (TreasuryDirect: EE bonds).

The minimum purchase is $25, and you can buy any amount above that to the penny, for example $36.73 (TreasuryDirect: I bonds). That makes them easy to fund in small, uneven amounts, which suits irregular income.

How I bond rates work

An I bond's rate is built from two parts. The fixed rate is set when you buy the bond and never changes for that bond. The inflation rate is reset every six months, so the overall rate changes too (TreasuryDirect: I bonds interest rates). The Treasury announces new rates every May 1 and November 1, and the inflation part is based on changes in the non-seasonally adjusted Consumer Price Index for all Urban Consumers (CPI-U), including food and energy (TreasuryDirect: I bonds interest rates).

For I bonds issued from May 1, 2026 to October 31, 2026, the combined rate is 4.26 percent, which includes a fixed rate of 0.90 percent (TreasuryDirect: I bonds). The Treasury shows how that number is calculated: fixed rate + (2 × semiannual inflation rate) + (fixed rate × semiannual inflation rate). With a 0.90 percent fixed rate and a 1.67 percent semiannual inflation rate, that works out to 4.26 percent (TreasuryDirect: I bonds interest rates). New rates are due November 1, so a bond bought after that date may have a different fixed rate and combined rate.

Two details are easy to miss. First, your bond's rate does not change on May 1 and November 1; it changes every six months from your bond's own issue month. A bond issued in January, for example, gets new rates each July 1 and January 1 (TreasuryDirect: I bonds interest rates). Second, if deflation is severe enough to push the combined rate below zero, the Treasury stops it at zero (TreasuryDirect: I bonds interest rates).

Fixed rates have varied a lot over time. They were 0.00 percent for bonds issued from May 2020 through October 2022, and 1.30 percent for bonds issued from November 2023 through October 2024 (TreasuryDirect: I bonds interest rates). That is why the fixed rate matters most if you plan to hold a bond for years.

How EE bonds work

EE bonds bought today earn a fixed rate set at purchase. For EE bonds issued from May 1, 2026 to October 31, 2026, that rate is 2.40 percent (TreasuryDirect: EE bonds). Since May 2005, new EE bonds earn their fixed rate for the first 20 years, and the Treasury may adjust the rate after that (TreasuryDirect: EE bonds).

The headline EE feature is a guarantee: for EE bonds bought now, the Treasury promises the bond will double in value in 20 years, adding money at the 20-year mark if needed (TreasuryDirect: EE bonds). Doubling in 20 years works out to roughly 3.5 percent a year compounded, which is more than the stated 2.40 percent, but you only get that benefit if you hold the bond the full 20 years.

Both bonds: how interest grows

I and EE bonds both earn interest monthly and compound it semiannually: every six months, the interest earned is added to the bond's principal, and the next six months of interest is calculated on that larger amount (TreasuryDirect: I bonds). Both earn interest for up to 30 years, and you receive the interest all at once when you cash the bond or when it matures (TreasuryDirect: Tax information for EE and I bonds).

The rules that trip people up

The 12-month lock. You cannot cash in an I or EE bond until you have held it for 12 months (TreasuryDirect: I bonds). Money you might need for rent next month, or for a quarterly estimated tax payment, is a poor fit.

The 5-year rule. If you cash a bond before it is 5 years old, you lose the last 3 months of interest. TreasuryDirect's own example: cash in after 18 months and you get the first 15 months of interest (TreasuryDirect: I bonds). Values shown in your TreasuryDirect account for bonds under 5 years old already leave out those final 3 months (TreasuryDirect: I bonds interest rates).

Purchase limits. In a calendar year you can buy up to $10,000 in electronic EE bonds and up to $10,000 in electronic I bonds for yourself as owner, in addition to what you buy for a child or as gifts (TreasuryDirect: Buying savings bonds). For I bonds, the limit applies per Social Security Number or per Employer Identification Number (TreasuryDirect: I bonds), and an electronic bond can be registered to a trust, estate, corporation, partnership or other entity (TreasuryDirect: Buying savings bonds). If you are thinking about buying through a business entity, that is a question for a tax professional, not a blog post.

Who can own them. A named owner must have a Social Security Number and be a U.S. citizen, a U.S. resident, or a civilian employee of the United States (TreasuryDirect: Buying savings bonds).

How the interest is taxed

Savings bond interest is subject to federal income tax but not state or local income tax (TreasuryDirect: Tax information for EE and I bonds). You can choose to report the interest every year or defer it until you actually receive it; TreasuryDirect says most people defer (TreasuryDirect: Tax information for EE and I bonds). If your bonds are in TreasuryDirect, your Form 1099-INT appears in your account by January 31 of the year after you receive the interest (TreasuryDirect: Tax information for EE and I bonds). Using the money for qualified higher education expenses may let you avoid federal tax on the interest, but the bonds must be in an adult's name, not the child's (TreasuryDirect: Buying savings bonds). IRS Publication 550 covers the details (IRS Publication 550).

Side-by-side

  • Rate type: I bonds combine a fixed rate with an inflation rate that resets every 6 months; EE bonds have a fixed rate for 20 years.
  • Rate for bonds issued May 1 to Oct 31, 2026: I bonds 4.26 percent (0.90 percent fixed); EE bonds 2.40 percent.
  • Special feature: I bonds adjust with CPI-U inflation; EE bonds are guaranteed to double in value at 20 years.
  • Shared rules: $25 minimum, 12-month lock, 3-month interest penalty before 5 years, 30-year life, $10,000 per year per type, federal tax only.

Questions to ask before you buy

  • Will I definitely not need this money for at least 12 months, and ideally 5 years?
  • Is my emergency fund already in an insured, easy-to-reach account?
  • Am I comparing the current rate with other options using today's numbers, not last year's?
  • Do I understand how I will report the interest at tax time?

The bottom line

I bonds are built to keep pace with inflation, while EE bonds trade a lower stated rate for a guaranteed double at 20 years. Both are sold directly by the U.S. Treasury, which calls EE bonds a low-risk way to save (TreasuryDirect: EE bonds), and both are simple to buy in small amounts, and illiquid for the first year. Read the current rates on TreasuryDirect before deciding, and talk to a qualified professional about your own situation.
