Solo 401(k) vs SEP-IRA for Side Hustle Income: A Plain-English Comparison

Intro: why freelancers compare these two plans

If you earn self-employment income from freelancing, digital products, consulting or another side business, two retirement plan names come up constantly: the SEP-IRA and the one-participant 401(k) (often called a Solo 401(k)). Both can support deductible retirement contributions based on business earnings. They are not the same product.

This post compares who qualifies, how the employee and employer pieces work, what the IRS publishes for recent limits, and how deadlines often differ. It is general education only — not tax, legal or personalized financial advice, and it does not promise any income or tax outcome. Confirm details in official IRS materials or with a qualified professional.

Who qualifies

SEP-IRA. The IRS describes a Simplified Employee Pension as a plan that lets an employer contribute to a traditional IRA (or a Roth SEP IRA when the plan allows it, under SECURE 2.0 rules for years after 2022) set up for each eligible employee. A business of any size — including a self-employed person with no staff — can establish a SEP. Investor.gov describes it the same way: an employer makes contributions into an IRA in the employee’s name.

If you later hire workers, a SEP generally must cover eligible employees who meet the plan’s age, service and compensation tests. The IRS model rules allow covering people who have reached age 21, worked for you in at least 3 of the last 5 years, and received at least the SEP minimum compensation for that year. You can use less restrictive rules, not more restrictive ones. See the IRS SEP plan overview and Publication 560.

Solo / one-participant 401(k). The IRS says this is a traditional 401(k) covering a business owner with no employees, or that person and his or her spouse. It follows the same basic rules as other 401(k) plans. The “no nondiscrimination testing” advantage disappears if you hire common-law employees who become eligible — they generally must be included. See one-participant 401(k) plans.

Employee vs employer contribution pieces

This is the biggest structural difference.

SEP-IRA — employer contributions only. Under a regular SEP, only the employer contributes. The IRS states that elective salary deferrals and catch-up contributions are not permitted in SEP plans (older SARSEP arrangements are a separate, closed category). For employees, the employer contribution generally cannot exceed the lesser of 25% of compensation or the annual dollar cap. For yourself as a self-employed person, Publication 560 requires a special calculation: compensation is net earnings from self-employment after subtracting one-half of self-employment tax and the contribution for yourself. Use the IRS worksheets rather than a rough percentage on Schedule C profit.

Solo 401(k) — employee deferrals plus employer contributions. Per the IRS, the owner can make:

  • Elective deferrals (as the employee) up to 100% of compensation (“earned income” if self-employed), up to the annual deferral limit; and
  • Employer nonelective contributions up to 25% of compensation as defined by the plan (again with the self-employed computation in Publication 560).

Total annual additions (not counting catch-ups) cannot exceed the overall defined-contribution limit. Elective deferral limits are by person, not by plan — deferrals into a day-job 401(k) count toward the same personal limit. The IRS explains that interaction on its 401(k) and profit-sharing contribution limits page.

2025 and 2026 contribution limits

Figures below come from the IRS COLA limits table, IR-2025-111, Publication 560, and the SEP contribution limits page.

  • Elective deferrals (Solo 401(k) employee piece): $23,500 for 2025; $24,500 for 2026.
  • Catch-up (age 50+, most 401(k) plans): $7,500 for 2025; $8,000 for 2026. For ages 60–63 under SECURE 2.0, the higher catch-up is $11,250 for 2025 and 2026 when the plan allows it.
  • Overall defined-contribution / SEP dollar cap (excluding catch-ups): $70,000 for 2025; $72,000 for 2026.
  • Compensation taken into account for percentage limits: $350,000 for 2025; $360,000 for 2026.
  • SEP reminder: a regular SEP uses that same overall dollar cap, but has no separate employee deferral or age-50 catch-up.

Your actual maximum also depends on earned income and the Publication 560 worksheets. If a number is unclear for your facts, omit it until you confirm it.

Setup and funding deadlines

SEP-IRA. You can set up a SEP for a year as late as the due date (including extensions) of your business income tax return for that year, and employer contributions for the year are due by that same deadline. See the IRS SEP FAQs and SEP plan page.

Solo 401(k). Publication 560’s key-rules table lists qualified plans as set up by the employer’s tax-filing due date (including extensions), with employer profit-sharing contributions due by that return deadline (including extensions). For elective deferrals, the IRS notes that owner/employee deferrals must be elected by the end of the tax year and can then be deposited by the tax-return filing deadline, including extensions. Publication 560 also notes that for 2023 and later years, a sole proprietor with no employees can adopt a section 401(k) plan after year-end by the tax filing deadline without regard to extensions, with special timing for first-year elective deferrals. Confirm the adoption and deposit calendar with your plan provider before you rely on a late setup.

Paperwork and features that differ

  • Annual filing: A one-participant 401(k) generally must file Form 5500-EZ if it has $250,000 or more in assets at year-end; plans under that threshold may be exempt. A SEP generally has no Form 5500 filing requirement for the employer.
  • Setup: A SEP can use IRS Form 5305-SEP or an approved prototype (do not file 5305-SEP with the IRS). A Solo 401(k) needs a written 401(k) plan document and a plan account.
  • Design: The IRS notes a self-employed 401(k) can be tailored to allow loans and hardship distributions, and elective deferrals may be pre-tax or designated Roth when the plan permits. A SEP is IRA-based and simpler; participant loans are not a SEP feature the way they can be under a 401(k).
  • Hiring later: Both plans change when you add eligible employees. A Solo 401(k) loses its “no testing” simplicity; a SEP may require contributions for every eligible employee under the same allocation formula.

Checklist: when each structure often fits

Use this as a thinking aid, not a recommendation.

  • You want the lightest paperwork and no Form 5500. A SEP often fits that description (model form; generally no annual 5500).
  • You want employee elective deferrals (and possibly catch-ups) on top of an employer contribution. That points toward a Solo 401(k), not a regular SEP.
  • You also defer into a W-2 job’s 401(k). The personal elective-deferral limit is shared across plans.
  • You might hire staff soon. Model SEP coverage rules or 401(k) eligibility and testing before you lock in a structure.
  • You are unsure of net self-employment income. Run the Publication 560 worksheets (or ask a tax professional) before deciding from headline dollar caps alone.
  • You care about Roth elective deferrals or plan loans. Check whether a given Solo 401(k) document allows them; a SEP follows IRA-based rules instead.

Conclusion

A SEP-IRA and a Solo 401(k) both help self-employed people save for retirement with tax-favored contributions, but they split the work differently: a SEP is employer-contribution only with late setup flexibility and light filing; a Solo 401(k) adds an employee deferral (and catch-up) layer, more design options, and possible Form 5500-EZ filing once assets grow. Confirm current limits on IRS.gov, read Publication 560, and treat this comparison as education — not advice about which plan you should open.
