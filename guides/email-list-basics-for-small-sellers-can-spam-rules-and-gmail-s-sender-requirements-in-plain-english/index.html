Intro: why an email list comes with rules

An email list lets you reach people directly instead of depending on a platform's algorithm. For a small digital-product seller, it might be a free printable that invites people to sign up, followed by occasional emails about new products.

But email is also regulated. In the U.S., the CAN-SPAM Act sets rules for commercial email, and the Federal Trade Commission enforces it [FTC, CAN-SPAM Guide]. On top of the law, major inbox providers set their own technical requirements. Gmail's, for example, apply to anyone sending to personal Gmail addresses [Google, Email sender guidelines].

This post summarizes both in plain English. It's an educational overview, not legal advice, and it doesn't cover laws outside the U.S.

Which emails CAN-SPAM covers

A common misconception is that CAN-SPAM only applies to bulk "spam." The FTC says it covers all commercial messages, which the law defines as email whose primary purpose is "the commercial advertisement or promotion of a commercial product or service," including email that promotes content on commercial websites [FTC, CAN-SPAM Guide]. There's no exception for business-to-business email. The FTC's example: a message to former customers announcing a new product line has to comply [FTC, CAN-SPAM Guide].

So a single email to past buyers announcing a new printable counts, not just large blasts.

The core CAN-SPAM requirements

The FTC's compliance guide lists the main requirements [FTC, CAN-SPAM Guide]:

  • Accurate header information. "From," "To," "Reply-To" and routing information must be accurate and identify who sent the message.
  • Honest subject lines. The subject line must accurately reflect the content.
  • Identify the message as an ad. You have leeway in how, but the disclosure must be clear and conspicuous.
  • Include a valid physical postal address. This can be a street address, a P.O. box registered with the U.S. Postal Service, or a private mailbox registered with a commercial mail receiving agency under Postal Service rules.
  • Tell people how to opt out. The explanation must be clear and easy for an ordinary person to understand. You can offer a menu of email types, but you must include an option to stop all marketing messages.
  • Monitor others who email for you. Hiring a company to send your emails doesn't transfer your legal responsibility. Both the company whose product is promoted and the company that sends the message can be held responsible.

The postal-address rule is one many solo sellers overlook. If you'd rather not publish your home address, the FTC guide's list of acceptable options includes a registered P.O. box or private mailbox.

Penalties are serious. According to the FTC, each separate email that violates the law can bring penalties of up to $53,088 [FTC, CAN-SPAM Guide].

Unsubscribes: the 10-business-day rule

The FTC is specific about opt-outs [FTC, CAN-SPAM Guide]:

  • Your opt-out method must keep working for at least 30 days after you send a message.
  • You must honor an opt-out request within 10 business days.
  • You can't charge a fee, ask for personal information beyond an email address, or require any step other than replying to an email or visiting a single web page.
  • Once someone opts out, you can't sell or transfer their address, except to a company you've hired to help you comply.
  • People with a subscription or membership with you still have the right to opt out of marketing emails.

The FTC also notes that your spam filter shouldn't block opt-out requests [FTC, CAN-SPAM Guide].

Receipts and updates vs. marketing emails

Not every email is commercial. The FTC describes "transactional or relationship" messages, which include content that confirms a purchase the person already agreed to, delivers goods or services they bought, or notifies them of changes to an account or subscription [FTC, CAN-SPAM Guide]. Messages that are only transactional are exempt from most of CAN-SPAM, though they still can't have false or misleading routing information.

The catch is mixed messages. If the subject line suggests a promotion, or the transactional part isn't mainly at the beginning, the FTC says the message counts as commercial [FTC, CAN-SPAM Guide]. A download-delivery email that opens with a big sale banner can become a marketing email in the law's eyes. Gmail gives similar practical guidance: don't include promotions in sales-receipt messages [Google, Email sender guidelines].

Gmail's sender requirements

Since February 1, 2024, Google has required all senders to personal Gmail accounts to meet certain standards [Google, Email sender guidelines]. For all senders, these include:

  • Set up SPF or DKIM email authentication for your sending domain.
  • Use a TLS connection to send email.
  • Keep the spam rate reported in Google's Postmaster Tools below 0.3%.
  • Format messages to the standard Internet Message Format.

Senders of more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail accounts face extra requirements, including SPF, DKIM and DMARC, and one-click unsubscribe plus a clearly visible unsubscribe link in marketing messages [Google, Email sender guidelines]. Google also recommends keeping the spam rate below 0.10% and never reaching 0.30% [Google, Email sender guidelines].

Most small sellers use an email service provider rather than their own mail server. Google says that if you do, you should verify that the provider authenticates your domain's email with SPF and DKIM [Google, Email sender guidelines].

Building the list the right way

Google's guidance on subscriptions lines up with good practice for any small list [Google, Email sender guidelines]:

  • Make sure people opt in, and confirm each address before subscribing it.
  • Don't buy email addresses from other companies.
  • Don't email people who didn't sign up.
  • Avoid pre-checked opt-in boxes that subscribe people automatically.
  • Consider removing subscribers who never open or read your messages, and periodically confirm people still want your emails.

Google also notes that messages from senders who send only to people who want them are less likely to be reported as spam, which protects your domain's reputation over time [Google, Email sender guidelines].

In practice, a small seller might offer a free printable in exchange for a sign-up, then clearly say on the form what kind of emails will follow.

A simple pre-send checklist

  • Is the "From" name and address accurate, and does it identify me?
  • Does the subject line honestly describe the email?
  • Is it clear the message is promotional, if it is?
  • Is my valid postal address (or registered P.O. box or private mailbox) included?
  • Is there a clear, working unsubscribe option that will keep working for at least 30 days?
  • Am I processing unsubscribes within 10 business days?
  • Is my sending domain authenticated with SPF or DKIM, ideally through my email provider?
  • Am I only emailing people who opted in?

Conclusion

An email list can be a steady, direct connection with people who like what you make, but it comes with clear rules. CAN-SPAM requires honest headers and subject lines, a postal address, an ad disclosure and a working opt-out that's honored within 10 business days. Gmail adds technical requirements such as authentication and low spam rates. Start small, email only people who asked to hear from you, and keep the FTC guide and Google's sender guidelines bookmarked, since both can change.
