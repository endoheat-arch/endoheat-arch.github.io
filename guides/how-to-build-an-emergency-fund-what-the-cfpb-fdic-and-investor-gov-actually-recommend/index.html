Why a cash cushion still matters

Unexpected bills do not wait for a convenient month. A car repair, a medical bill, a broken appliance, or a stretch without income can push a household toward credit cards or loans that cost more than the original expense. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) notes that people who struggle to recover from a financial shock often have less savings left for the next one, and that relying on credit can turn a one-time hit into lasting debt (CFPB, An essential guide to building an emergency fund).

That gap shows up in national survey data. In the Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking, 63 percent of adults said they would cover a hypothetical $400 expense using cash or its equivalent. Only 55 percent said they had set aside enough for three months of expenses in an emergency or "rainy day" fund (Federal Reserve, Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025 — Savings and Investments).

This post is a plain-English how-to drawn from the CFPB, the FDIC, and Investor.gov. It is general education, not personal financial advice. Your situation, income, and risk tolerance are unique.

What counts as an emergency fund

The CFPB defines an emergency fund as a cash reserve specifically set aside for unplanned expenses or financial emergencies. Common examples include car repairs, home repairs, medical bills, or a loss of income. In general, the money is meant for large or small unplanned bills that are not part of your routine monthly spending (CFPB, An essential guide to building an emergency fund).

Investor.gov frames the same idea as "rainy day" savings: enough money in a safe, accessible place to cover an emergency such as sudden unemployment, so you are not forced into high-interest debt (Investor.gov, Save for a Rainy Day; Investor.gov, Build Wealth Over Time Through Saving and Investing).

How much to aim for (and why there is no single number)

The CFPB is clear that the right amount depends on your situation. It suggests looking at the unexpected expenses you have actually faced and what they cost, then using that history to set a goal. Even a small cushion can help if you live paycheck to paycheck or have irregular income (CFPB, An essential guide to building an emergency fund).

Official sources often cite a multi-month buffer as a longer-term target. The FDIC notes that financial experts generally recommend at least six months of living expenses in a federally insured product such as a savings account or certificate of deposit (CD), so you can weather a major income drop or a large repair (FDIC, Saving for the Unexpected and Your Future). Investor.gov similarly says some people keep up to six months of income in savings for emergencies (Investor.gov, Save for a Rainy Day).

Treat those figures as orientation, not a mandate. Start with a first milestone you can reach, then build. Tracking essentials (housing, utilities, food, transportation, insurance, minimum debt payments) in a simple monthly planner can make the math concrete. Five practical ways to build it

The CFPB lays out several strategies that work alone or together (CFPB, An essential guide to building an emergency fund):

  • Create a savings habit. Set a specific goal, pick a consistent contribution (a fixed amount each payday, for example), monitor progress, and celebrate milestones so the habit sticks.
  • Manage cash flow. Watch when money comes in and when bills leave. Adjusting due dates with creditors, or moving extra into savings in weeks when cash is looser, can free room to save.
  • Use one-time windfalls. Tax refunds, bonuses, or cash gifts can jump-start the fund if you put all or part of them aside instead of spending everything.
  • Make saving automatic. Recurring transfers from checking to savings are one of the easiest ways to stay consistent. Watch balances so the transfer does not trigger overdraft fees.
  • Save through work. If you have direct deposit, ask whether you can split each paycheck between checking and a savings account so a portion is set aside before you see it.

The FDIC uses a similar automation example: putting $20 into savings every other week adds up to $520 a year before interest (FDIC, Saving for the Unexpected and Your Future). Investor.gov also recommends automatically depositing a set amount into a bank or credit union savings account each pay period (Investor.gov, Build Wealth Over Time Through Saving and Investing).

Where to keep the money so it stays safe and usable

The CFPB wants the fund safe, accessible, and harder to spend on non-emergencies. Options it lists include a dedicated bank or credit union account, a prepaid card you load in advance, or cash kept carefully (with the caveat that cash can be stolen, lost, or destroyed) (CFPB, An essential guide to building an emergency fund).

The FDIC recommends a traditional FDIC-insured savings account for many savings goals because you can withdraw when needed, earn some interest, and often face withdrawal limits that reduce casual spending. It also notes that CDs can hold emergency savings, but early withdrawal usually carries a penalty (FDIC, Saving for the Unexpected and Your Future).

Deposit insurance matters. FDIC coverage is automatic for deposit products at insured banks. Savings accounts, checking accounts, money market deposit accounts, and CDs are examples of insured deposits. For a single account owned by one person, coverage is generally up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category (FDIC, Are My Deposit Accounts Insured by the FDIC?). Credit union deposits may be insured by the NCUA; Investor.gov notes both FDIC and NCUA as the insurance backstops for many savings products (Investor.gov, Save for a Rainy Day).

Investor.gov also reminds savers of the tradeoff: money in savings is typically safer and more available than invested money, but it may earn a lower rate that does not always keep up with inflation. That is why many people keep an emergency cushion in savings and invest separately for longer-term goals (Investor.gov, Save for a Rainy Day). Stocks, bonds, and mutual funds are not FDIC-insured, and their values can fall (FDIC, Saving for the Unexpected and Your Future).

When to use it (and when not to)

Write your own rules before you need them. The CFPB suggests deciding what counts as an emergency, staying consistent, and remembering that not every surprise is a crisis—but an uncovered medical bill can still be a legitimate use. The point of the reserve is to avoid credit cards or loans that add interest and fees on top of the original cost. If you do spend the fund, rebuild it (CFPB, An essential guide to building an emergency fund).

A simple starter checklist

  • Open or designate a separate savings account at an FDIC- or NCUA-insured institution.
  • List your essential monthly costs and pick a first savings milestone you can reach.
  • Set an automatic transfer or paycheck split for a fixed amount you can afford.
  • Park any refund or bonus portion you can spare into the same account.
  • Write down what counts as an emergency for your household.
  • After any withdrawal, restart contributions until the balance is restored.
