Intro: turning existing photos into a digital product

If you already take decent photos, even on a phone, stock photography is one way to turn them into a digital product that you upload once and license repeatedly. Adobe Stock is one of the larger marketplaces, and its contributor program is free to join [Adobe Stock Contributor].

This guide sums up what Adobe's own contributor pages say about eligibility, royalties, file specs, releases and AI content. It isn't a promise of earnings. Your results depend on what you shoot, how buyers search and how much competition there is.

Who can contribute

Adobe says you can sign up as a contributor with your Adobe ID and start uploading at no cost, as long as you have high-quality photos, videos or vector content and own all the rights [Adobe Stock Contributor]. You must be at least 18 years old and the sole owner of every file you upload for sale [Adobe Stock Contributor].

Adobe's account guidelines add a few setup items: you must accept the Contributor Agreement, enter your tax information and payment method in the Contributor Portal, and make sure your name, email address and country of residence are accurate and match your legal documents [Adobe Help, Account guidelines].

The arrangement is non-exclusive. Adobe describes it as a partnership that lets it promote and license your content while you stay in control of your work [Adobe Stock Contributor].

How royalties work

According to Adobe's royalty page (last updated July 13, 2026), standard royalty rates apply to content licensed under Adobe Stock plans, including subscriptions, credit packs and on-demand licenses [Adobe Help, Royalty rates]:

  • Photos, vectors and illustrations: 33%
  • Video: 35%

The rate is applied to the net price per licensed item, based on the U.S. purchase price and including any discounts. Adobe's own example: if a plan costs $29.99 a month for 10 photo licenses, the royalty would be about $0.99 per licensed photo ($29.99 / 10 x 33%) [Adobe Help, Royalty rates].

For Adobe's large subscription plans (350 or more standard assets a month), Adobe lists minimum royalties per licensed standard asset that rise with a contributor's lifetime licenses: $0.33 for 0 to 999, $0.36 for 1,000 to 9,999, and $0.38 for 10,000 and above [Adobe Help, Royalty rates]. Content licensed through custom agreements and some non-standard plans, such as Adobe Express plans, may instead earn 33% (35% for video) of an attributable amount, or a flat rate [Adobe Help, Royalty rates].

The takeaway: individual licenses often earn well under a dollar, so stock is a volume and patience game, not a quick-cash one.

Technical requirements for photos

Adobe says it accepts photos shot with any camera, including a mobile device, as long as they meet its standards [Adobe Help, Photo requirements]. Its listed specs are:

  • Format: JPEG
  • Color space: sRGB
  • Resolution: at least 4 megapixels, up to 100 megapixels
  • File size: up to 45 MB

[Adobe Help, Photo requirements]

Adobe also notes that buyers are businesses, agencies, design studios and marketers looking for content with commercial value [Adobe Help, Photo requirements]. Its account guidelines describe accepted content as high resolution, well composed and free of watermarks or compression artifacts [Adobe Help, Account guidelines].

Model and property releases

This is the area beginners most often skip. Adobe says a model release is a legal agreement between the contributor and the person shown, giving permission to publish, distribute and license the content for commercial use [Adobe Help, Model releases]. You need one whether you work with professional models or capture candid moments in public [Adobe Help, Model releases].

Adobe's test is broad: if a person can be recognized in any way, by face, voice, tattoos, clothing or even specific surroundings, you need their consent before submitting [Adobe Help, Model releases]. If your content contains recognizable private property, you also need a signed property release [Adobe Stock Contributor].

In the Contributor Portal, release tools live under the Uploaded Files tab, in the Releases area. You can collect a signature digitally with Adobe Acrobat Sign using the signer's email address, or upload a scanned release, which must be a JPEG [Adobe Help, Upload a release].

A simple way to start without releases: shoot subjects with no identifiable people or private property, such as food, textures, plants, desk setups and everyday objects without logos.

Generative AI content: labeling and rights

Adobe Stock accepts images, vectors and videos created with generative AI, with specific rules [Adobe Help, Generative AI FAQ; Adobe Help, Generative AI guidelines]:

  • You must have all necessary rights to submit the content, and Adobe tells contributors to check the terms of any AI tool they use to make sure they can submit its output for licensing.
  • You must always check the Created using generative AI tools box for AI content.
  • If AI content depicts, is based on or is meant to portray an identifiable real person, such as by using their photo as a prompt or naming them, it requires a model release.
  • If the content isn't based on a real person but looks like one, you must check the People and Property are fictional box.

[Adobe Help, Generative AI guidelines]

Titles, keywords and avoiding spam flags

Adobe says its search engine prioritizes the first 10 keywords on each asset, so put your best keywords first. It can suggest a title and up to 25 keywords, which you should review and edit [Adobe Help, Photo requirements].

Adobe is also clear about what not to do. Don't submit multiple images with minimal changes in angle or depth, or the same photo with different edits. Long, repetitive or irrelevant titles and keywords can also be treated as spam. Adobe says spamming may lead it to block or permanently close your account [Adobe Help, Photo requirements].

A realistic starting plan

  • Audit what you have. Pick 20 to 30 of your sharpest photos with clear subjects and no recognizable people, logos or private property.
  • Check the specs. Export as sRGB JPEGs of at least 4 MP.
  • Think like a buyer. Ask what a blog, ad or presentation could use the image for. "Overhead flat lay of a budget planner and coffee" is more useful than "my desk."
  • Write honest metadata. Use a short, descriptive title and put the 10 most relevant keywords first.
  • Shoot in themes. Small, varied sets around one theme are easier to find than random one-offs, as long as each image is truly different.
  • Collect releases before you photograph people or private places, not after.
  • Track and adjust. Note which subjects get accepted and licensed, and make more of those.

Conclusion

Adobe Stock gives photographers a free, non-exclusive way to license their work, with royalties of 33% for photos and 35% for video under standard plans. Per-license earnings are small, so treat it as a long-term portfolio. Get the basics right (specs, releases, AI labeling and clean metadata) and you'll avoid the most common reasons new contributors run into trouble.
