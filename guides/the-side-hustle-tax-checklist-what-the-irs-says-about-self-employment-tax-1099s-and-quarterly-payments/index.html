Intro: why side income deserves its own checklist

Selling printables, freelancing on weekends, doing deliveries or running a small online shop can all bring in real money. The part many beginners put off is taxes, usually because nobody withholds anything from side income the way an employer does from a paycheck.

This checklist walks through the basics using the IRS's own plain-language pages, so you know which questions to ask before tax season. It's general education about U.S. federal rules, not personal tax, legal or financial advice. Your situation (state, filing status, other income) can change the answer, so check with a qualified tax professional if you're unsure.

Check 1: Know that side income counts, even without a form

The IRS is direct about this. Income from gig and side work must be reported on your tax return even if it's part-time, temporary or side work, even if it isn't reported on a form like a 1099 or W-2, and even if you were paid in cash, property, goods or virtual currency, according to the IRS Gig Economy Tax Center.

The IRS's list of gig work includes selling goods online and providing creative or professional services, so digital product sellers and freelancers are covered too (IRS: Gig economy tax center).

  • Treat every sale or client payment as income you'll need to account for.
  • Don't wait for a form to arrive before you start tracking.

Check 2: Learn the $400 self-employment line

According to the IRS, you must file a tax return if you have net earnings from self-employment of $400 or more from gig work, even if it's a side job (IRS: Manage taxes for your gig work). The same $400 figure is the point at which you must pay self-employment tax and file Schedule SE (IRS: Self-employment tax).

Note the word net: that's generally your earnings after business expenses, which is one more reason to keep receipts (see Check 6). The IRS also notes these rules apply no matter how old you are, even if you already receive Social Security or Medicare (IRS: Self-employment tax).

Check 3: Understand what self-employment tax is

Self-employment tax is Social Security and Medicare tax for people who work for themselves, similar to what's withheld from most employees' pay. The IRS lists the rate as 15.3%: 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare (IRS: Self-employment tax).

Two details worth knowing:

  • You figure it on Schedule SE, not through an employer.
  • You can deduct the employer-equivalent portion of your self-employment tax when calculating your adjusted gross income; the IRS notes this deduction affects income tax only, not the self-employment tax itself (IRS: Self-employment tax).

This tax is separate from regular income tax, which is why side income can feel like it's taxed "twice." Budgeting for both from the start avoids a surprise in April.

Check 4: Know which 1099 forms might show up (and the new thresholds)

Two forms matter most for side hustlers, and both had threshold changes from the law the IRS calls the One, Big, Beautiful Bill.

Form 1099-K comes from payment apps and online marketplaces. The IRS says these platforms are required to file one when payments for goods or services exceed $20,000 and the number of transactions exceeds 200 (IRS: Form 1099-K FAQs). But the IRS also says you may still get one for smaller amounts, and your state may have a lower reporting threshold (IRS: Form 1099-K FAQs). Either way, you must report all income from selling goods or services, whether or not a form arrives (IRS: Understanding your Form 1099-K).

Form 1099-NEC reports payments to non-employees for services, such as freelance clients paying you. The IRS says the reporting threshold is $2,000 for payments made after December 31, 2025, up from $600 (IRS: Form 1099-NEC and independent contractors), and it may be adjusted for inflation beginning in 2027 (IRS: Instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC).

If a business does send you a form, the IRS says you should generally receive your copy by January 31 (IRS: Manage taxes for your gig work). Higher thresholds mean fewer forms, not less taxable income.

Check 5: Decide how you'll handle estimated taxes

Because nobody withholds tax from side income, the IRS says you generally need to make estimated tax payments if you're in business for yourself, and individuals generally have to pay if they expect to owe $1,000 or more when they file (IRS: Estimated taxes).

The four due dates for a calendar year are April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15 of the following year (IRS: Estimated tax FAQ). For 2026, the 2026 Form 1040-ES lists the last payment as January 15, 2027, and says you don't have to make it if you file your 2026 return by February 1, 2027 and pay the entire balance due (IRS: 2026 Form 1040-ES).

Options the IRS describes:

  • Pay quarterly using the Form 1040-ES worksheet, and refigure it if your income estimate changes (IRS: Estimated taxes).
  • Pay more often (weekly or monthly) as long as enough is paid by the end of each quarter (IRS: Estimated taxes).
  • Withhold more from a day job by filing a new Form W-4, if you're also an employee (IRS: Manage taxes for your gig work).

On penalties, the IRS says most taxpayers generally avoid the underpayment penalty if they owe less than $1,000 after withholding and credits, or if they paid at least 90% of the current year's tax or 100% of the prior year's tax, whichever is smaller, with special rules for some higher-income taxpayers (IRS: Estimated taxes).

Check 6: Set up a simple record-keeping habit

You don't need fancy software. The IRS says you may choose any recordkeeping system suited to your business that clearly shows your income and expenses, and that you must keep records as long as needed to prove the income or deductions on a return (IRS: Recordkeeping). It also notes that you carry the "burden of proof" for deductions you claim (IRS: Recordkeeping).

A low-effort routine:

  • Save every receipt (a phone photo works) in one folder per month.
  • Log each sale or client payment with the date, amount and platform.
  • Write down platform and payment fees, since sales reports and payouts often differ.
  • Do a 15-minute monthly check-in to total income, expenses and what you set aside for taxes.

Check 7: Be honest about hobby vs. business

Not every money-making activity is a business in the IRS's eyes. Its factors include whether you keep complete and accurate books, whether you change methods to improve profitability, how much time and effort you put in, and whether you've made or expect to make a profit (IRS: Know the difference between a hobby and a business).

It matters because, per the IRS, if you're not trying to make a profit you can't use a loss from the activity to offset other income, and income from a not-for-profit activity is reported on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 8 (IRS: Know the difference between a hobby and a business).

Check 8: Know the forms and free filing help

For independent contractors, the IRS lists Form 1040, Schedule C (profit or loss from business) and Schedule SE (self-employment tax) as the core forms (IRS: Manage taxes for your gig work). The same page points to free options such as IRS Free File and Volunteer Income Tax Assistance (VITA) sites, and to Form 1040-X if you need to correct a return after forgetting gig income.

A quick printable version of the checklist

  • I'm tracking all side income, with or without a 1099.
  • I know whether my net self-employment earnings could reach $400.
  • I'm budgeting for self-employment tax on top of income tax.
  • I know which 1099-K or 1099-NEC forms I might receive, and that I report income either way.
  • I've picked a plan for estimated taxes (quarterly, more often, or extra W-4 withholding).
  • My next estimated payment date is on my calendar.
  • I have one place for receipts and one log for income and fees.
  • I've thought honestly about whether this is a hobby or a business.
  • I know which forms I'll file and where to get free help.

Conclusion

Side income taxes are mostly about habits: track everything, set money aside, and know your dates. None of this guarantees a particular result, and rules can change, so check the IRS pages below each year and talk to a tax professional about your own situation.
