Sole Proprietor or LLC? What a Side Hustle Structure Actually Changes (and What It Doesn't)

The moment a side hustle starts bringing in money (a first printable sale, a first freelance invoice) a familiar question shows up: "Do I need an LLC?" The internet tends to answer with either "yes, immediately" or "never bother." The official guidance from the U.S. Small Business Administration and the IRS is more useful than either, because it explains what a business structure actually changes.

This post compares the two structures most solo side hustlers weigh: the sole proprietorship and the single-member LLC. It is general education based on SBA, IRS and FinCEN materials. It is not legal, tax or financial advice, rules vary by state, and the right choice depends on your situation. A licensed attorney or tax professional can help with your specific case.

The default: you are probably already a sole proprietor

You don't have to file anything to become a sole proprietor. The SBA explains that you're "automatically considered to be a sole proprietorship if you do business activities but don't register as any other kind of business." The IRS defines a sole proprietor as "someone who owns an unincorporated business by themselves."

The trade-off is liability. According to the SBA, a sole proprietorship does not create a separate business entity, so your business assets and liabilities are not separate from your personal ones, and "you can be held personally liable for the debts and obligations of the business." The SBA also says sole proprietorships "can be a good choice for low-risk businesses and owners who want to test their business idea before forming a more formal business."

What an LLC is

A limited liability company is "a business structure allowed by state statute," per the IRS, and each state may regulate it differently. Owners are called members, and most states permit single-member LLCs.

The SBA describes the main draw: LLCs "protect you from personal liability in most instances," meaning personal assets such as your vehicle, house and savings generally aren't at risk if the LLC faces bankruptcy or lawsuits. The SBA suggests LLCs "can be a good choice for medium- or higher-risk businesses, owners with significant personal assets they want protected," and owners who want to avoid corporate tax rates.

Side-by-side comparison

  • How it starts. A sole proprietorship begins automatically when you do business (SBA). An LLC is formed by registering with your state, and the SBA notes an LLC needs a registered agent in that state before filing. Filing fees vary by state.
  • Personal liability. The SBA comparison table lists sole proprietorships as "unlimited personal liability" and LLC owners as "not personally liable," with the caveat that rules vary by state and the table is "intended only as a guideline" (SBA).
  • Federal income tax. By default, a single-member LLC is "disregarded as separate from its owner" for income tax, unless it files Form 8832 to be taxed as a corporation (IRS). For an individual owner, its activity generally goes on Schedule C, the same form a sole proprietor uses (IRS).
  • Self-employment tax. The IRS says an individual owner of a single-member LLC running a trade or business "is subject to the tax on net earnings from self employment in the same manner as a sole proprietorship" (IRS).
  • Tax ID numbers. A disregarded single-member LLC with no employees and no excise tax liability doesn't need an EIN for federal tax purposes and uses the owner's name and SSN or EIN instead (IRS). An LLC needs its own EIN if it has employees or certain excise tax filings.
  • Operating in other states. An LLC that does business in more than one state might need to file for foreign qualification, and the SBA notes foreign-qualified businesses typically pay taxes and annual report fees in both states (SBA).

Five common myths

Myth 1: "An LLC lowers my taxes." Under the default rules, a single-member LLC owned by an individual is taxed on the owner's return, and self-employment tax applies the same way it does for a sole proprietor (IRS). Different tax treatment requires an election, such as filing Form 8832, and that's a conversation for a tax professional.

Myth 2: "An LLC protects me from everything." The SBA's wording is "in most instances." Its insurance guidance adds that LLC status "can protect your personal property from lawsuits. However, that protection has limits," and that business insurance can fill gaps (SBA).

Myth 3: "I need an LLC before I can sell anything." Nothing in the SBA guidance requires it. Doing business without registering another structure simply makes you a sole proprietor by default (SBA).

Myth 4: "I have to pay someone for an EIN." The IRS says its online EIN application is free, and warns: "Beware of websites that charge for an EIN. You never have to pay a fee for an EIN" (IRS).

Myth 5: "Every new LLC must file a beneficial ownership report with FinCEN." That rule changed. FinCEN says its final rule, effective August 14, 2026, exempts U.S. companies from beneficial ownership information (BOI) reporting, so they "are no longer required to file BOI reports" (FinCEN). Be wary of anyone who says you owe a BOI filing fee for a U.S. LLC.

What either structure still needs

Separate money. The SBA recommends opening a business bank account "as soon as you start accepting or spending money as your business," noting it offers "limited personal liability protection by keeping your business funds separate from your personal funds" (SBA). The SBA also notes a DBA plus an EIN can let a sole proprietor open one (SBA).

A name strategy. If you sell under a brand rather than your legal name, you might need to register a DBA ("doing business as") name with your state, county or city. The SBA says a DBA "doesn't provide legal protection by itself, but most states require you to register your DBA if you use one" (SBA).

Tidy records. Whichever structure you pick, income and expenses still flow to your tax return. A simple monthly money check-in, like the one built into the Calm Money Monthly Planner, makes it easier to see what the side hustle is actually earning before you decide whether formalizing it is worth the cost.

A short pre-decision checklist

  • Write down what could realistically go wrong in your business (a client dispute, a product claim, a physical injury) and how serious it would be.
  • Look up your state's LLC formation fee, annual report fee and any registered agent requirement on your secretary of state's website.
  • Check whether you'll be doing business in more than one state.
  • Price business insurance either way, since the SBA treats it as a separate layer of protection.
  • Open a separate business bank account, whichever structure you choose.
  • If you're considering a tax election or have significant personal assets, talk to a licensed attorney or CPA before filing anything.
  • Get any EIN directly from IRS.gov, which is free.

The bottom line

For many solo side hustles, the real difference between a sole proprietorship and a default single-member LLC is liability and paperwork, not federal income tax. The SBA frames the sole proprietorship as a reasonable way to test an idea and the LLC as a fit for higher-risk work or owners with more to protect. Start from your own risk, check your state's rules, and keep business money separate from day one.
