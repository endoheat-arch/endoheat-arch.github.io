How to Use an Assisted Living Move In Checklist (What to Pack, Tour Questions and Move Day)

Originally published on Telegraph

Helping a parent move into a community is equal parts logistics and emotion. An assisted living move in checklist keeps the practical side on paper so you are not inventing the next step from memory at midnight. This guide covers a simple timeline, tour questions, what to pack (and what to leave), room-by-room downsizing, move day, and the first month — without pretending this is medical, legal or financial advice.

Below is a practical system you can use with the kit or with your own notebook.

1. Start your assisted living move in checklist with a shared timeline

Before you tour a single hallway, put dates on a single page the whole family can see. A workable senior living move in checklist usually runs backward from a target move month:

  • Early: Talk with your parent about wishes and must-haves (privacy, pets, location near family, faith services, outdoor space). Write their words down, not your assumptions.
  • 2–3 months out: List communities to tour, set a rough monthly budget range, and choose one family lead for calls and paperwork.
  • 6–8 weeks: Tour at least two or three places with the same question list so you can compare apples to apples.
  • 4–6 weeks: Narrow the choice, read the agreement carefully, note deposit and move-in dates, and measure the new room.
  • 2–4 weeks: Book movers or helpers, start downsizing with your parent's input, and update address plans.
  • 1 week: Pack familiar comforts, label clothing, confirm move time and who will stay for the first meal.
  • Move day and first month: Set up the room first, then watch how the first weeks feel and adjust.

Copy each row onto a deadline tracker with a "done" box. Shared checklists reduce sibling arguments because the next step is visible, not debated from three different texts.

2. Questions to ask assisted living facilities on every tour

A nice lobby is not a care plan. Take the same questions to ask assisted living facilities to every visit, and write answers while you are still in the parking lot:

  • Monthly cost and what it includes (meals, laundry, housekeeping, activities)
  • Extra fees and how prices change when care needs go up
  • Levels of help available, and how the community decides when someone needs more
  • Staff on site day and night, and how night call buttons work
  • Meals, menus, dining times and whether trays can go to the room
  • Activities, outings and how residents learn about them
  • Room sizes, what furniture you can bring, and what is already provided
  • Visiting hours, guest meals and overnight policies
  • Transport to medical appointments
  • How families get updates (calls, portal, care conferences)
  • Move-in steps, assessments, waitlists and average wait times
  • How new residents are helped to settle in during the first weeks

Rate the "feel" of each place (1–5) for both your parent and the family. A community comparison chart with three columns (Option A / B / C) makes the decision meeting shorter and kinder. If something is unclear in the brochure, ask admissions to email a fee sheet and a sample agreement before you put money down.

3. What to pack for assisted living (and what to leave behind)

Space is smaller than a family home. What to pack for assisted living should favor daily use, safety and familiarity over "maybe someday" items. A practical what to bring to assisted living starter list:

Clothing and personal care

  • A week of easy outfits, labeled; shoes that fit and are hard to trip in
  • Seasonal outerwear that matches closet space
  • Toiletries the community allows; a labeled toiletry caddy
  • Glasses, hearing aids, chargers and spare batteries in a labeled pouch

Familiar comforts

  • Favorite pillow and blanket, a few framed photos, a small lamp if allowed
  • A clock that is easy to read, a calendar and a notepad
  • One chair or small piece of furniture that fits the measured room
  • Hobby items that still get used (books, puzzles, knitting, music player)

Paperwork to carry on move day (not buried in a box)

  • Photo ID, insurance cards (including any Medicare or supplemental cards)
  • Medication list copied from the bottles, plus the pharmacy phone number
  • Doctor contacts and recent appointment notes you have been asked to bring
  • Signed residency paperwork copies and emergency contacts

What usually stays behind or goes to family

  • Duplicate furniture that will not fit
  • Large collections, heavy tools and kitchen gear the community already provides
  • Valuables you are not ready to leave in a shared building (decide together on jewelry, cash and important papers)
  • Items your parent does not recognize or no longer wants — forcing "keep everything" makes the new room feel like a storage unit

Label clothing with name tags before move day. Communities wash laundry in batches; unlabeled socks disappear.

4. Use a room-by-room downsizing planner before the boxes appear

Downsizing is the emotional bottleneck. Do it room by room with your parent present whenever possible. For each item, choose one lane: bring, family, sell or donate. Write the decision on a downsizing planner so siblings are not reopening closed boxes later.

Tips that keep the peace:

  • Start with low-emotion rooms (linen closet, garage, guest room) before the bedroom and living room
  • Photograph sentimental pieces that will not fit, then let the original go if that feels right
  • Give adult children a deadline for picking up "family" piles so the house does not stall
  • Schedule donation pickup and an estate-sale or thrift run on the calendar, not "someday"

You are not erasing a life. You are choosing what travels into the next chapter so the new room can breathe.

5. How to move into assisted living without losing the paperwork trail

How to move into assisted living is as much about documents as boxes. Keep a who-to-call sheet with the community desk, admissions coordinator, care lead, doctor, pharmacy, siblings, movers, donation pickup and utilities at the old home. Next to it, a deadline tracker for:

  • Residency agreement (signed copy)
  • Deposit and first payment
  • Admission and assessment paperwork
  • Doctor forms the community requested
  • Address changes (mail, bank, benefits)
  • Old home lease notice or sale steps
  • Utilities stopped or transferred
  • Mover booking confirmation
  • Belongings inventory

Family meeting and admissions call scripts help when emotions run high. Write the opening line, the questions and the closing recap ("who is doing what by when") before you dial. After each call, log the person's name, any reference number and the next step.

6. Build a move-day checklist and a first-night bag

Move day goes smoother when the room is set up before the hallway fills with boxes. A short move-day checklist:

  • Confirm arrival window with the community and movers
  • Pack a first-night bag: meds, glasses, hearing aids, phone charger, change of clothes, toiletries, snacks your parent likes
  • Hang clothes and make the bed first so the space looks like a bedroom, not a warehouse
  • Place photos and the familiar lamp where they are visible from the chair or bed
  • Stay for a meal if welcome, introduce yourselves to staff on duty, and write down the names you meet
  • Leave copies of the medication list and emergency contacts at the desk if asked

One calm adult should be the "point person" for staff questions so your parent is not answering the same thing five times while tired.

7. Track costs before and after the move

Money stress multiplies when nobody writes numbers down. Use a simple budget page with two columns: before move and after move for housing, meals, help/care services, utilities, transport and personal items. Add a one-time list for deposit, movers, new bedding, boxes and cleaning or clear-out of the old home.

This page is for family clarity, not for tax or benefits decisions. Rules about deductions, Medicaid, veterans benefits and contracts vary widely. Read the community's fee sheet with someone you trust, and take professional advice when money or legal documents are involved.

8. Use the first 30 days as a soft landing, not a final grade

The first month is when small fixes matter. Keep a first-30-days page with a row per week: how they seem, what is going well, what to adjust and whether you checked in with staff. Visit often early if you can. Ask what would make the room feel more like home. Notice meal times, sleep and how activities land — then ask staff for ideas instead of guessing alone.
