Intro: why index funds attract so many myths

If you've started reading about investing alongside your side hustle, you've probably seen index funds described as the simple, "set it and forget it" option. Simple is a fair goal, but a lot of the shorthand online skips important details. This post walks through seven common myths and checks each one against plain-language guidance from the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's investor education site, Investor.gov.

This is general education, not personal financial, tax or investment advice. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy any particular fund, and no investment is guaranteed to make money.

Myth 1: An index fund is its own kind of investment

An index fund isn't a separate product category so much as a strategy. The SEC describes an index fund as a mutual fund, exchange-traded fund (ETF) or unit investment trust that follows a passive strategy designed to achieve approximately the same return as a particular index before fees (Investor.gov glossary: Index Fund).

A market index measures the performance of a "basket" of securities meant to represent a part of the market or economy, and you can't invest in an index directly. Index funds offer an indirect way to do it (SEC Investor Bulletin: Index Funds). The same bulletin names the S&P 500, the Russell 2000 and the Wilshire 5000 Total Market Index as examples of indexes that funds may track.

It also matters how an index is built. Many indexes are weighted by market capitalization, so bigger companies make up a bigger share of the index, while some, such as the Dow Jones Industrial Average, are weighted by share price (SEC Investor Bulletin: Index Funds).

Myth 2: Index funds can't lose money

They can. The SEC says plainly that, like any investment, index funds involve risk, and an index fund is subject to the same general risks as the securities in the index it tracks (SEC Investor Bulletin: Index Funds). Mutual funds aren't guaranteed or insured by the FDIC or any other government agency, and you may lose some or all of the money you invest (Investor.gov: Mutual Funds).

Index funds can also be less flexible than other funds. The SEC notes an index fund may have less flexibility than a non-index fund to react to price declines in the securities in its index (SEC Investor Bulletin: Index Funds).

Diversification helps, but it isn't a shield. Investor.gov says diversification can't guarantee your investments won't suffer if the market drops, though it can improve the chances that losses are smaller (Investor.gov: Diversify Your Investments).

Myth 3: Every index fund is cheap

Index funds often cost less, and there's a real reason. Because managers aren't actively picking securities, they don't need research analysts to help choose investments, which could mean lower overall costs to shareholders (SEC Investor Bulletin: Index Funds). Passive management usually means less trading, lower transaction costs and lower fees than actively managed funds (Investor.gov glossary: Index Fund).

But "usually" isn't "always." The same SEC bulletin warns that not all index funds have lower costs than actively managed funds, and says to be sure you understand the actual cost of any fund before investing (SEC Investor Bulletin: Index Funds).

The place to check is the fund's prospectus. Its fee table shows the expense ratio, which is the percentage of the fund's average net assets used each year to pay operating expenses (Investor.gov glossary: Expense Ratio). The SEC also cautions that some funds advertise low or zero expense ratios without mentioning other costs you might pay, such as broker commissions (SEC Investor Bulletin: Mutual Fund and ETF Fees and Expenses).

Myth 4: An index fund matches its index exactly

Not quite. The SEC lists tracking error as a risk: a fund may not perfectly track its index, for example if it holds only a sample of the index's securities. An index fund may also underperform its index because of fees and expenses, trading costs and tracking error (SEC Investor Bulletin: Index Funds). Notice the phrase "before fees" in the SEC's definition, too. The goal is approximately the index's return before costs come out (Investor.gov glossary: Index Fund).

Myth 5: A small fee difference doesn't matter

This is the one worth remembering. Investor.gov shows a hypothetical $100,000 investment growing 4% a year for 20 years. With a 0.25% annual fee it ends at about $208,000, with a 0.50% fee about $198,000, and with a 1.00% fee about $179,000 (Investor.gov: Understanding Fees).

The reason is that ongoing fees don't just shrink your balance. You also lose any return you would have earned on the money paid in fees (SEC Investor Bulletin: How Fees and Expenses Affect Your Investment Portfolio). As Investor.gov puts it, a fund with high costs must perform better than a low-cost fund to generate the same returns for you (Investor.gov: Mutual Funds). To compare real funds, Investor.gov points readers to FINRA's free Fund Analyzer tool.

Myth 6: ETFs and mutual funds are basically the same thing

They share a lot. Both pool investors' money, are typically managed by SEC-registered advisers, can follow passive or active strategies, and are liquid (SEC Investor Bulletin: Characteristics of Mutual Funds and ETFs). The differences are practical, though:

  • How you buy them. You can buy mutual fund shares from the fund itself, but you need a brokerage account to buy, sell and hold ETF shares.
  • How they're priced. Mutual fund orders are filled at the net asset value (NAV) calculated at the end of the business day, so you won't know your exact price until then. ETFs trade at market prices throughout the day, and that price can be above or below the ETF's NAV.
  • Taxes in a taxable account. Many ETFs typically have fewer capital gains distributions than mutual funds because of how they're structured. In a tax-advantaged account like a 401(k) or IRA, the SEC says there's no tax difference between the two.

All three points come from the same SEC bulletin on mutual funds and ETFs, which also notes you can't always tell a fund's type from its name.

Myth 7: Anything with "index" in the name works the same way

Newer "non-traditional" index funds track custom-built indexes instead of broad market indexes. The SEC describes smart beta funds, which select investments using factors such as value, dividends or quality, and quant funds, which use numerical methods or algorithms, and warns some of these funds may be complex and hard to understand (SEC Investor Bulletin: Smart Beta, Quant Funds and Other Non-Traditional Index Funds). Read how the index is built before assuming a fund behaves like a broad market fund.

Questions to ask before you buy any fund

The SEC suggests reading the fund's prospectus and most recent shareholder report, both free on the fund's website or the SEC's EDGAR database, and asking these questions (SEC Investor Bulletin: Index Funds):

  • What fees and expenses can I expect to pay for buying, owning and selling this fund?
  • What specific risks are associated with this fund?
  • How is the makeup of the fund's index determined?
  • How does the fund's investment strategy fit with my investment goals?

Conclusion

Index funds are popular for understandable reasons, but they still carry market risk, their costs vary, and the details of the index and fund structure matter. Reading the fee table and the risk section takes a few minutes and answers most of the myths above.
