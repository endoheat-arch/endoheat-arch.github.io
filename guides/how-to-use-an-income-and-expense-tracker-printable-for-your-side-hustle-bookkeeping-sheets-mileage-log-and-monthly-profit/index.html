How to Use an Income and Expense Tracker Printable for Your Side Hustle (Bookkeeping Sheets, Mileage Log and Monthly Profit)

Originally published on Telegraph

If your side hustle's "bookkeeping" is a shoebox of receipts, a few PayPal emails and a vague memory of what you spent at the craft store, you're not alone. An income and expense tracker printable is the simplest fix: one page for money coming in, one page for money going out, and a quick total at the end of each month. This guide walks through setting up paper bookkeeping sheets in about twenty minutes, keeping them current in five minutes a week, and turning them into a monthly profit number you can actually read.

Below is a practical system you can use with the kit or with your own ruled paper.

1. Set up your income and expense tracker printable in one binder

Paper bookkeeping only works if everything lives in one place. Before you record a single number, put together a simple binder:

  • A 1-inch binder or a sturdy folder with pockets
  • Tab dividers labeled Income, Expenses, Invoices, Mileage, Inventory and Summaries
  • Twelve copies of the income and expense pages (one set per month), or print the first three months and add more as you go
  • A zip pouch or envelope for paper receipts, labeled with the current month
  • One pen that stays with the binder

Write the month and year at the top of each page as soon as you print it. Undated pages are flexible, but a blank date line is how January and February end up mixed together.

If you sell in more than one place (a market table, Etsy, a Payhip shop, freelance clients), add a short "Source" note in each income row. You'll thank yourself later when you want to know which channel is actually worth your time.

2. Printable bookkeeping sheets: what to record on every line

A row on an income log should answer, at a glance, who paid, for what, how much and how. Record these on every sale:

  • Date the money arrived (not the date you sent the invoice)
  • Customer or platform, such as a client's name or "Payhip"
  • Invoice or order number, so you can match the row to an email or receipt
  • Amount received, and if a platform takes a fee, note the fee in the expense log or in a separate column so it isn't lost
  • Payment method: PayPal, card, cash, bank transfer

The expense log mirrors it: date, vendor, what you bought, category (supplies, shipping, software, ads, equipment), amount and a receipt number. Number your paper receipts with the same number you wrote on the row, then drop them in the month's envelope. Matching numbers make it easy to find a receipt months later.

Keep personal spending off these printable bookkeeping sheets. If you buy groceries and printer ink in one trip, write down only the ink. Mixing personal and business costs is the fastest way to make the numbers meaningless.

3. Track who still owes you with an invoice tracker

If you invoice clients, money can sit unpaid for weeks without anyone noticing. A separate invoice tracker keeps that visible:

  • Invoice number, client, date sent and amount
  • Due date, written in a way you'll notice (circle it or use a highlighter)
  • Date paid, filled in only when the money actually arrives

Once a week, scan the "date paid" column for blanks past their due date. That's your follow-up list. When an invoice gets paid, copy it onto the income log for that month so your income totals stay complete.

4. Mileage log printable: capture trips when they happen

Driving to a craft fair, the post office, a client meeting or a supply store adds up. The problem is that nobody remembers mileage accurately three months later. A mileage log printable works best when it lives where the driving happens:

  • Keep a copy on a clipboard in the car or in the glove box
  • Write the date, starting point, destination, purpose and miles for each business trip
  • Note the odometer reading at the start and end of each month, too
  • Move the page into the binder at month-end

Rules about what counts as deductible business mileage, and how it's calculated, depend on where you live and your situation. A log just makes sure the information exists if you or a tax professional need it. IRS Publication 583 (Starting a Business and Keeping Records) is a good plain-language starting point on recordkeeping if you're in the US.

5. Use an inventory sheet if you sell physical products

Makers and resellers often know their sales but not their stock. A simple inventory sheet lists each product with its cost to make or buy, the selling price, how many are on hand and a reorder point. When you notice an item is close to its reorder point, you restock before a market weekend instead of after it.

Digital-only sellers can skip this page, or reuse it to track product ideas and their prices.

6. Profit and loss template for small business: close each month in ten minutes

This is where the tracker pays off. At the end of each month, sit down with the binder and a calculator:

  • Add up every row on the income log. That's your total income for the month.
  • Add up every row on the expense log, and if it helps, subtotal by category.
  • Subtract expenses from income. That's your rough monthly profit (or loss).
  • Write all three numbers on the monthly summary page, along with one sentence about what happened ("Fall market weekend; bought new display table").

A one-page profit and loss template for small business owners doesn't have to be fancy. The value is in doing it every month, so you can see trends: which months are busy, which costs keep creeping up, and whether a new product is pulling its weight.

At year-end, copy each month's totals onto an annual summary page. Twelve rows give you the whole year on one sheet, which makes tax-season prep, or a conversation with whoever helps with your taxes, much less painful.

7. A five-minute weekly small business bookkeeping routine

The biggest mistake with any tracker is filling it in once a quarter. Pick one regular time each week, like Sunday evening, and run through this short list:

  • Check your payment emails and platform dashboards, and add any new sales to the income log
  • Empty your wallet and bag of receipts, number them and log them
  • Look at the invoice tracker for anything overdue
  • Move this week's mileage page entries into the binder if the clipboard is full

That's the whole routine. Small, regular small business bookkeeping sessions beat a stressful weekend every April.
