Side Hustle Mileage Deduction in 2026: How the IRS Standard Mileage Rate Works (and When Actual Expenses Apply)

If you freelance, sell digital products, or run a small local service, the miles you drive for client meetings, supply runs, and post office trips can add up. The IRS gives self-employed people two ways to figure car expenses, and the choice you make in your first year can limit your options later.

This guide walks through what IRS publications say about the standard mileage rate, the actual expense method, and the records you need. It is educational only, not tax advice. Your situation may differ, so check the official sources linked here or talk to a qualified tax professional.

Who can deduct car expenses

According to IRS Topic No. 510, Business Use of Car, most employees cannot deduct unreimbursed car expenses. There are exceptions, and being self-employed is one of them. If you use the car for both business and personal driving, you can deduct only the business part.

The IRS says you can generally use one of two methods: the standard mileage rate or actual expenses. If you qualify for both, the IRS suggests working out your deduction both ways before choosing (Topic No. 510).

The 2026 standard mileage rates

2026 has two business rates, not one. The IRS standard mileage rates page lists:

  • January 1 to June 30, 2026: 72.5 cents per business mile
  • July 1 to December 31, 2026: 76 cents per business mile

The mid-year change came from Announcement 2026-11, which raised the business rate "due to recent increases in the price of fuel." The higher rate covers business driving costs paid or incurred on or after July 1, 2026, and the original rate from Notice 2026-10 still covers driving before that date (IRB 2026-29). A Congressional Research Service report notes that this kind of mid-year change has happened only four times since 2000: in 2005, 2011, 2022, and 2026 (CRS, IRS Standard Mileage Rates).

So log the date of every trip. You'll need to split your 2026 business miles into the two periods and apply the right rate to each. Here's a made-up example of the math: 400 business miles in spring at 72.5 cents works out to $290, and 400 miles in the fall at 76 cents works out to $304.

Who is allowed to use the standard mileage rate

Topic No. 510 says you must own or lease the car. You also must not have:

  • Operated five or more cars at the same time, as in a fleet operation
  • Claimed depreciation on the car using any method other than straight-line, or used MACRS
  • Claimed a Section 179 deduction or the special depreciation allowance on the car
  • Claimed actual expenses for a car you lease

Timing matters. For a car you own, you must choose the standard mileage rate in the first year the car is available for use in your business. After that, you can switch between the standard rate and actual expenses from year to year. For a leased car, choosing the standard rate means you keep it for the whole lease period, including renewals (Topic No. 510).

What you can still deduct on top of the rate

The standard rate doesn't cover everything. According to Publication 463:

  • Parking fees and tolls for business are deductible on top of the standard rate. Parking at your own regular place of work is a nondeductible commuting expense.
  • Car loan interest: if you're self-employed, you can deduct the business-use share of the interest on Schedule C. Pub 463's example is 60% of the interest for a car used 60% for business.
  • State and local personal property taxes on the vehicle: the business part can go on Schedule C.

The actual expense method in brief

With actual expenses, you add up what it really costs to run the car and deduct the business share. Topic No. 510 lists gas, oil, repairs, tires, insurance, registration fees, licenses, and depreciation or lease payments. If you also drive the car personally, Pub 463 says you can split the costs based on miles driven for each purpose (Publication 463).

Depreciation is where this method gets more complicated. Topic 510 says that if you used the standard rate in the year the car was placed in service and switch to actual expenses later, you must use straight-line depreciation over the car's estimated remaining useful life. Depreciation limits also apply (Topic No. 510).

Business miles vs. commuting miles

This rule trips up a lot of side hustlers. Publication 463 says driving between your home and your main or regular place of work is personal commuting, no matter how far it is.

Pub 463 also describes cases where daily trips can count, including when "your residence is your principal place of business" and you drive between home and another work location in the same business. Many digital-product sellers and freelancers work from home, so this exception can matter for them. Whether your home qualifies as your principal place of business depends on IRS rules, so check Publication 463 and the related home office guidance before you rely on it.

What a good mileage log looks like, according to the IRS

Topic No. 510 says the law requires you to back up your expenses "by adequate records or by sufficient evidence to support your own statement." For car expenses, Publication 463 (Table 5-1) says your records should show:

  • The mileage for each business use and your total miles for the year
  • The date of each use
  • Your business destination
  • The business purpose

The IRS also says:

  • Write it down close to the time. Records made "at or near the time" carry more weight. A log you update weekly counts as timely kept.
  • Digital logs are fine. A record kept on a computer is considered adequate.
  • One entry can cover a whole trip. A round trip or uninterrupted business use can be one record. A minor personal stop, such as lunch between two business stops, doesn't break it up.
  • Keep records for at least 3 years, generally counted from the date you file the return that claims the deduction.

Pub 463 includes a sample daily mileage log with columns for date, destination, business purpose, starting and ending odometer readings, and miles per trip (Publication 463). Where the deduction goes on your return

Sole proprietors report car expenses on Schedule C, line 9, and complete Part IV of Schedule C unless they have to file Form 4562 for depreciation (Publication 463; Topic No. 510).

Side-hustle mileage checklist

  • Decide in the car's first business year whether to use the standard rate. Choosing actual expenses first can rule out the standard rate later.
  • Log the date, destination, purpose, and miles for every business trip.
  • Record your odometer reading at the start and end of the year to get total miles.
  • Split 2026 miles at June 30 and July 1 (72.5 vs. 76 cents).
  • Keep receipts for business parking and tolls. You can deduct them on top of the standard rate.
  • Don't count regular commuting miles.
  • If you use actual expenses, keep receipts for gas, repairs, insurance, and the other costs, and figure your business-use percentage.
  • Keep your records for at least 3 years after filing.

Conclusion

For many freelancers and small online sellers, the standard mileage rate is the simpler way to deduct car costs: log your miles, multiply by the IRS rate, and add business parking and tolls. Actual expenses take more records and come with depreciation rules. Whichever method you use, a dated mileage log is what supports the deduction. Rates and rules change, so check IRS.gov before you file, and ask a tax professional if you aren't sure.
