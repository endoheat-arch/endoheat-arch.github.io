What Really Happens to a $5 Printable Sale on Payhip: Fees, Taxes and Payouts in Plain English

Originally published on Telegraph

Outline

When you sell your first digital download, it's tempting to think of the price as what you earn. A $5 printable feels like $5 in your pocket. In reality, a few small pieces come out between the buyer's click and your balance, and knowing what they are makes it much easier to price with confidence and keep clean records.

This post walks through those pieces for Payhip, the platform I use for printables like the Calm Money Monthly Planner. Everything here comes from Payhip's own pricing and help pages, plus official processor and government sources linked at the end. Rates change, so check the linked pages before you rely on any number.

This is general information, not financial, legal or tax advice. For your specific situation, talk to a qualified professional.

Payhip's three plans

Payhip publishes three plans on its pricing page:

  • Free Forever: $0 per month plus a 5% transaction fee.
  • Plus: $29 per month plus a 2% transaction fee.
  • Pro: $99 per month with no transaction fee.

Payhip says all three plans include all features, unlimited products and unlimited revenue. The plans don't unlock different tools. You're choosing how you'd rather pay: a percentage of each sale, a monthly subscription, or a mix of both.

For someone just starting out with a handful of low-priced printables, that structure matters. On the free plan you pay nothing until something sells. A monthly plan only starts to make sense once your sales volume is high enough that the lower percentage saves more than the subscription costs. You can work out that break-even point yourself with your own numbers. Divide the monthly fee by the percentage-point difference in transaction fees. For example, $29 ÷ 3% (the gap between 5% and 2%) comes to about $967 in monthly sales before Plus beats Free on fees alone.

The payment processor's cut

The Payhip fee isn't the only one. Payhip's pricing FAQ states plainly that PayPal and Stripe "will still charge at their standard rates once they complete a transaction," and that this applies on every plan (Payhip pricing). Customers can pay with PayPal or a card, according to the same page.

Stripe lists its standard rate for domestic cards in the US as 2.9% + 30¢ per successful transaction on its pricing page. PayPal publishes its own fee tables on its business fees page. Those rates vary by transaction type and country, so look up the row that matches your sales instead of assuming one figure.

That fixed 30¢ is the part that surprises people selling cheap products. On a $50 course it barely registers. On a $3 printable it's a meaningful slice of the sale.

Walking through a $5 sale

Here's an illustration using the published rates above. It assumes the free Payhip plan and a US card processed through Stripe at its standard domestic rate. Your actual numbers may differ.

  • Sale price: $5.00
  • Payhip free-plan fee (5%): $0.25
  • Stripe standard fee (2.9% + 30¢): about $0.45
  • Left before any income tax: about $4.30

So in this example, roughly 86% of the sticker price reaches you. Run the same math on a $3 product and the fees take a bigger share, because the 30¢ doesn't shrink with the price. This is one reason many sellers bundle several small printables into a single, slightly higher-priced product. Whether that fits your shop is a judgment call. The math just shows you where the pennies go.

As for timing, Payhip says it deposits sales "immediately after a transaction has been completed" (Payhip pricing). The money lands in your connected PayPal or Stripe account, and from there it follows that processor's own payout rules.

Sales tax and VAT: what Payhip handles

Taxes on digital goods can sound intimidating, especially once buyers in other countries show up. Payhip handles a good deal of this.

EU and UK VAT. Payhip's help center explains that EU law since January 1, 2015 requires VAT on digital items based on the customer's location, and that the UK has a similar rule. By default, Payhip handles digital EU and UK VAT for you. Under Settings → Taxes you can choose to add VAT on top of your price or include it in the price, which means you absorb it (Payhip: Digital EU VAT & UK VAT). Buyers outside the EU and UK aren't charged this tax.

US sales tax. According to Payhip's help center, starting July 1, 2026, Payhip handles US sales tax on sellers' behalf as a "marketplace facilitator." That means Payhip, not the creator, collects and pays it. Payhip says it doesn't charge sellers anything for this. You can also set a tax category per product, since some states tax digital products and others don't. There's one catch Payhip calls out: if you have a physical presence in a state where Payhip collects sales tax for you, that state may still expect a "zero dollar return" from you. Payhip suggests asking a tax professional whether that applies (Payhip: US Sales Tax).

Your own income tax side

Payhip handling sales tax and VAT doesn't cover your income tax. Those are separate things. In the US, the IRS Self-Employed Individuals Tax Center explains that you generally must file a return if your net earnings from self-employment were $400 or more, and that a return may still be required below that in some cases. The same page explains self-employment tax and estimated payments. How any of this applies to you depends on your situation, which is exactly what a tax professional is for.

Simple habits for tracking it all

You don't need fancy software to stay organized. A few low-effort habits go a long way:

1. Record gross and net separately. Write down the sale price and what actually arrived. The gap between them is your fees. 2. Download your reports monthly. Platform and processor dashboards let you export transaction history. A monthly export is far easier than reconstructing a year in April. 3. Revisit your plan choice occasionally. Use the break-even math above with your real sales instead of guesses. 4. Keep a simple budget for the business. A one-page monthly view of what came in and what went out is plenty at the start. If you like paper, the Calm Money Monthly Planner is the printable I made for exactly this kind of calm, monthly check-in.

Understanding where each dollar goes won't make a product sell. It does take the mystery out of your numbers, and that makes every pricing decision easier.
