Home Office Deduction for Side Hustlers: Simplified vs Regular Method Explained

Originally published on Telegraph

Outline

  • Who qualifies (exclusive and regular use, principal place of business)
  • The employee W-2 caveat after the TCJA
  • Simplified method ($5 per square foot, 300 sq ft cap)
  • Regular method (Form 8829, business percentage, direct vs indirect expenses)
  • Depreciation and recapture when you sell
  • Income limit on the deduction
  • Switching methods year to year
  • Recordkeeping and a short checklist
  • Sources

If your side hustle runs from a spare bedroom or garage studio, the home office deduction can reduce taxable business profit—if you meet IRS tests and pick a calculation method you can support. This post compares the simplified option and the regular method using Publication 587 (2025) and Topic no. 509. It is general education for preparing 2025 returns—not personal tax advice. Check current IRS pages or a tax professional for your facts. Nothing here promises income or a refund.

Who qualifies

Under Pub. 587 and Topic 509, you generally must use part of your home:

  • Exclusively and regularly as your principal place of business for a trade or business
  • Exclusively and regularly as a place where you meet patients, clients, or customers in the normal course of business
  • As a separate structure not attached to the home, used exclusively and regularly in connection with your trade or business
  • On a regular basis for certain storage of inventory or product samples (special rules; exclusive use may not be required)
  • For rental use, or as a daycare facility (each with its own rules)

Exclusive use means a specific area used only for the business—not also for personal recreation. The space can be a room or other identifiable area; it does not need a permanent partition (Pub. 587). Regular use means more than incidental or occasional use (Pub. 587).

Your home can qualify as a principal place of business if you use it exclusively and regularly for administrative or management activities (billing, books, ordering supplies, appointments, reports) and you have no other fixed location where you conduct substantial administrative or management activities for that business (Pub. 587; Topic 509).

The employee W-2 caveat

The IRS simplified-option page states that taxpayers can no longer claim a deduction for use of a home office as an employee, because miscellaneous itemized deductions for employee business expenses were eliminated for tax years beginning after 2017—even if the simplified method is used (IRS simplified option). Pub. 587’s flowchart likewise routes “using the part of your home as an employee” to no deduction (Pub. 587).

A W-2 day job plus a separate qualifying self-employed side hustle is different from remote work for an employer alone; confirm with current IRS guidance or a tax pro (Pub. 587).

Simplified method

For Pub. 587 (2025) and the IRS simplified option page (reviewed March 2026), the safe harbor is:

  • $5 per square foot of qualified business-use space
  • Cap of 300 square feet
  • So the rate × footage is at most $1,500 before the gross-income limit (300 × $5)

Claim it on Schedule C by entering home and office square footage rather than filing Form 8829 (Topic 509). Per the IRS comparison (simplified option page): home-related itemized deductions (mortgage interest, real estate taxes) stay in full on Schedule A; there is no home depreciation (and no later recapture) for simplified years; amounts above the gross-income limit may not be carried over; and a prior-year regular-method loss carryover may not be claimed in a simplified year. Business expenses unrelated to use of the home (supplies, advertising, equipment depreciation) remain deductible on normal Schedule C lines (Pub. 587).

Regular method (Form 8829)

Schedule C filers who do not elect the simplified method figure the deduction on Form 8829 (Topic 509; Pub. 587).

Business percentage. Compare business area to the whole home (square footage is common; equal-sized rooms can use a room count) (Pub. 587).

Direct vs indirect expenses (Pub. 587):

  • Direct — only for the business part (for example, painting only the office): generally deductible in full (subject to the overall limit)
  • Indirect — for the whole home (insurance, utilities, general repairs): deductible by the business percentage
  • Unrelated — only personal areas (lawn care, painting a non-business room): not deductible as home-office expenses

Owners may claim depreciation on the business portion (not land). For a home first used for business in 2025, Pub. 587 treats that part as nonresidential real property under MACRS—straight line over 39 years, mid-month convention (Pub. 587).

Depreciation and sale of the home

Under the regular method, depreciation reduces basis. If business use is within the home, Pub. 587 generally does not require allocating the sale for the home-sale exclusion—but you cannot exclude gain equal to depreciation allowed or allowable after May 6, 1997 (Pub. 587). Regular method: depreciation can be recaptured on sale; simplified: no recapture for years that option is used (simplified option page).

Income limit (both methods)

Neither method lets the home-office deduction exceed gross income from the business use of the home, reduced by business expenses unrelated to use of the home (Pub. 587; Topic 509).

  • Regular method: excess may be carried over to a later year (subject to that year’s limit)
  • Simplified method: excess may not be carried over

Switching methods year to year

Choose simplified or regular for any taxable year on a timely filed original return. Once chosen, you cannot switch for that same year; changing in a later year is allowed and does not require IRS consent as a change in accounting method (simplified option page; Pub. 587). After a simplified year, switching to regular means using the appropriate optional MACRS depreciation table (simplified option page).

Recordkeeping

Records must show the business area, exclusive and regular use (or an exception), and expenses or depreciation for that part. Keep receipts and home-basis records. Retention is generally the later of three years from the return due date or filing date, or two years after the tax was paid (Pub. 587). A monthly habit—square footage notes, utility bills, repair receipts—helps; the Calm Money Monthly Planner supports planned-vs-actual tracking so year-end allocation is less guesswork.

Short checklist

  • Confirm exclusive and regular use (or a storage/daycare exception) and the principal-place, client-meeting, or separate-structure path (Pub. 587; Topic 509)
  • Do not assume a W-2-only home office qualifies (IRS simplified option)
  • Measure square footage; simplified uses $5 up to 300 sq ft under current Pub. 587 (Pub. 587)
  • For regular method: separate direct vs indirect expenses; use Form 8829 (Pub. 587)
  • Apply the gross-income limit; only regular method generally allows carryover (Topic 509)
  • Lock in simplified vs regular on the original return for that year (simplified option page)
  • Keep space notes, bills, and basis records; read Pub. 587 or consult a tax professional before filing

The bottom line

For a qualifying side hustle, the IRS offers a $5-per-square-foot simplified option (up to 300 square feet under Pub. 587 for 2025 returns) with lighter recordkeeping and no home depreciation for those years, or Form 8829’s regular method with actual expenses, a business percentage, and possible depreciation—and possible depreciation-related gain later. Both share the same qualification tests and a gross-income limit; only the regular method generally allows carryover. Use IRS sources or a licensed professional for your facts.
