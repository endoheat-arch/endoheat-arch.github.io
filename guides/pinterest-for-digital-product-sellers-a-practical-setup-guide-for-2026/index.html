Intro: why Pinterest fits printables and templates

If you sell digital products such as planners, checklists or templates, you need a steady way for buyers to find them. Marketplace search is one route. Pinterest is another, because people use it to save ideas and plans, and each Pin can link to a page outside Pinterest, including a product page.

This guide covers the setup steps Pinterest itself documents, plus a simple workflow for linking Pins to products. It's an educational overview, not a promise of traffic or sales.

Pinterest's audience, by the numbers

Pinterest reported 640 million global monthly active users in the second quarter of 2026, up 11% year over year [Pinterest, Q2 2026]. Of those, 106 million were in the U.S. and Canada [Pinterest, Q2 2026]. Pinterest defines a monthly active user as an authenticated user who visits its website, opens its app or uses one of its browser or site extensions, such as the Save button, at least once in a 30-day period [Pinterest, Q2 2026].

A large audience doesn't mean your Pins will be seen. It means the people who search for things like "weekly meal planner" or "budget printable" are there; your job is to make Pins that match what they look for.

Step 1: Set up a free business account

Pinterest offers a free business account, which adds access to Pinterest Analytics and the Business Hub, plus the ability to run ads [Pinterest Help, Business account]. You can create a new business account or convert an existing personal account. A converted account keeps its Pins and followers, but business accounts are public, so a private personal account has to be switched to public before converting [Pinterest Help, Business account].

Pinterest notes that, at the time of writing, a new business account can only be created from a desktop device [Pinterest Help, Business account].

Step 2: Decide whether you can claim a website

Claiming a website links your Pinterest profile to Pins saved from that site. When an image from a claimed site is saved, your profile picture appears with the Pin, the Pin links to your profile and people see an option to follow you [Pinterest Help, Claim your website].

There's an important limit for digital-product sellers. To claim a website, you must own the domain and be able to edit its source code. Pinterest says that's why you can't claim most social accounts or stores hosted on marketplaces like Etsy, eBay and Amazon [Pinterest Help, Claim your website]. The same logic applies to a storefront on a shared domain you don't control.

What that means in practice:

  • If you have your own domain or blog, claim it using one of Pinterest's methods: an HTML tag, an HTML file or a DNS TXT record (Google Merchant Center is available for some accounts) [Pinterest Help, Claim your website].
  • If you only sell on a hosted store, you can still create Pins that link to your product pages. You just won't get the claimed-site attribution.

Step 3: Understand rich Pins (and when they apply)

Rich Pins automatically sync information from your site. Pinterest supports three types: article, recipe and product [Pinterest Help, Rich Pins]. Article rich Pins show the headline, description and author of a blog post. Product rich Pins show up-to-date price, availability and product details [Pinterest Help, Rich Pins].

To enable them, the pages need the right meta tags, such as Open Graph or Schema.org markup. Pinterest says it can take up to 24 hours for site content to sync [Pinterest Help, Rich Pins]. Product rich Pins only show product information if the product is in stock, the price is accurate and you meet Pinterest's merchant guidelines [Pinterest Help, Rich Pins]. Pinterest also notes that sites hosted by Etsy, Teachers Pay Teachers or eBay don't need extra markup [Pinterest Help, Rich Pins].

If your store platform doesn't let you edit page tags, rich Pins may not be available for those pages. That's fine; standard Pins still link out.

Step 4: Design Pins that point to one product

A simple, repeatable Pin workflow:

  • One Pin, one destination. Link each Pin directly to the product page or a blog post about it, not a general homepage.
  • Show the product. For printables, a clear mockup of a page or two tells people what they'll get.
  • Say what problem it solves. "Plan a week of dinners on one page" is clearer than a product code.
  • Write a plain description. Describe what's included and who it's for, in words a buyer would actually search.
  • Make more than one Pin per product. Different images and headlines for the same page let you see which angle gets saved or clicked.

For example, my Weekly Meal Planner and Calm Money Monthly Planner on Payhip each have their own Pins that link straight to the product page.

Step 5: Organize boards around buyer problems

Boards group your Pins and help people browse. Instead of one board called "My Shop," try boards named for what buyers are trying to do: "Meal Planning Printables," "Budget Planner Ideas," "Freelancer Client Templates." Mix your own Pins with genuinely useful related content so the board is worth following.

Step 6: Measure, then adjust

A business account gives you Pinterest Analytics to review how your Pins perform [Pinterest Help, Business account]. Check which Pins get saves and outbound clicks, then make more Pins in the style that works. Compare that with your store's own sales data, since a Pin with many saves but no clicks may need a clearer headline or image.

Treat the first few months as an experiment. Pinterest's scale [Pinterest, Q2 2026] is an opportunity, not a guarantee.

Disclosure and honesty basics

If you Pin products you get paid to promote, such as affiliate links or brand partnerships, the FTC's guidance applies. It says that if you have a financial, employment, personal or family relationship with a brand, you should disclose it clearly when you endorse its products [FTC, Disclosures 101]. For your own products, make sure mockups and descriptions accurately show what a buyer receives.

Conclusion

Pinterest can be a useful, steady discovery channel for digital products, but it rewards setup and consistency. Start with a free business account, claim your website if you own one, add rich Pin markup where your platform allows, and make clear Pins that each lead to one specific product. Then let your analytics, not guesswork, tell you what to make next.
