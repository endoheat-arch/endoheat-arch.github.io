New Family Caregiver? What to Do in the First Week

Originally published on Telegraph

One phone call can turn you into a caregiver overnight. A parent falls, a partner gets a hard diagnosis, and suddenly you're juggling appointments, pharmacies, insurance, and your own job. You don't have to solve everything this week. You just need a few things written down so nothing important slips.

Print it, put it in a binder, and fill it in as you go.

Below is the plan I'd follow in week one, whether or not you use the kit.

1. Start one place for everything

Pick a single binder, folder, or notebook and keep every note, letter, and business card in it. When a nurse or office asks a question, you'll know where the answer is. Scattered sticky notes and phone screenshots are how details get lost.

2. Write a one-page care snapshot

Put the basics on one page: full name, date of birth, diagnoses you've been told about, allergies, doctors and their phone numbers, pharmacy, and insurance member numbers. Keep a copy in the binder and a photo on your phone. This is the page you'll hand to a new doctor, an urgent-care desk, or a family member who's covering for you.

3. Copy the medication list straight from the labels

Write each medication exactly as it appears on the bottle: name, strength, how often, and who prescribed it. Add over-the-counter pills and supplements too. Bring the list to every appointment and ask the doctor or pharmacist to review it. Don't change doses on your own; the list is there so the professionals can see the full picture.

4. Log every appointment and call

After each visit or phone call, jot down the date, who you spoke with, what they said, and what happens next. For insurance and billing calls, always write the representative's name and the reference number. That one habit can save hours when a claim is denied or a referral goes missing.

5. Ask the right people the right questions

A few calls usually come up early:

  • The doctor's office: "What should we watch for, and who do we call after hours?"
  • The insurer: "What's covered, does this need prior authorization, and what's my reference number?"
  • Your employer or HR: "What leave or flexible-schedule options do I have?" In the US, ask whether the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) applies to you.

6. Share the load before you burn out

Make a simple weekly schedule and ask family and friends to take specific jobs: one person drives to Tuesday's appointment, another picks up groceries, another handles the pharmacy refill. Specific asks get far more yeses than "let me know if you need anything."

7. Track the money and the deadlines

Caregiving costs add up fast: copays, mileage, supplies, and sometimes paid help. Keep a simple monthly list of what's spent and by whom. Next to it, track deadlines like insurance appeals, benefit forms, and prescription renewals so nothing expires quietly.

8. Check in on yourself

Put your own sleep, meals, and one small break on the weekly plan. Caregivers who never rest tend to get sick themselves. If you're overwhelmed, your local Area Agency on Aging (in the US, search the Eldercare Locator) can point you to respite care and support groups.

9. Find the important papers early

Before a crisis forces it, find out where the key documents are kept. That usually means the insurance cards (including any Medicare or supplemental cards), a photo ID, a recent list of doctors, and any paperwork naming who can speak for your person if they can't speak for themselves. Many families discover too late that nobody knows whether those papers exist. You don't need to fill out anything new this week; just ask, gently, "If something happens, where would I find your papers?" and write the answer down. If documents are missing or confusing, a qualified attorney or the care team's social worker can explain the options where you live.

10. Keep a short "what changed" note

Things can shift quickly in the first weeks: a new prescription, a different appointment time, a change in how your person is eating or sleeping. Keep a running note at the front of the binder with the date and one line about what changed. When a doctor asks "When did this start?", you'll have an answer instead of a guess. It also helps siblings and helpers catch up without a long phone call.

A first-week checklist you can copy

  • One binder or folder started, with everything going into it
  • Care snapshot page written, copied, and photographed
  • Medication list copied from the labels and shown to the pharmacist
  • Appointment and call log started, with names and reference numbers
  • After-hours contact number confirmed with the doctor's office
  • Leave or schedule options asked about at work
  • Two or three specific helper jobs handed out for next week
  • Where-are-the-papers question asked and the answer written down
  • One small break for yourself on the calendar

If you only get half of these done, that's still a good week. The point is to stop holding everything in your head.
