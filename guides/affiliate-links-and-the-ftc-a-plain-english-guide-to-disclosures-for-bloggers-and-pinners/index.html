Intro: disclosure is part of the job

If you earn a commission when someone buys through your link, or a brand sends you a free product, your readers deserve to know. In the U.S., that idea is the core of the Federal Trade Commission's Endorsement Guides. The FTC says endorsements must be honest and not misleading, and that a connection between an endorser and a marketer that a significant minority of consumers wouldn't expect should be disclosed "clearly and conspicuously" [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].

This post walks through what the FTC's own guidance says, in plain English, for people adding affiliate links to blog posts, pins or social posts. It is an educational summary, not legal advice. The FTC itself notes that its staff guidance isn't a safe harbor and that context matters [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].

What the Endorsement Guides are (and aren't)

The FTC brings endorsement cases under Section 5 of the FTC Act, which generally prohibits deceptive advertising. The Guides explain how the FTC thinks about endorsements, but the Guides themselves don't have the force of law. Practices inconsistent with them can still lead to enforcement actions alleging Section 5 violations [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ]. The Guides were revised in 2023, including a new definition of "clearly and conspicuously" [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].

The FTC says it applies the same rules to influencers and bloggers as to traditional reviewers. The question is always whether the audience understands the relationship [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ]. When it does take action, the FTC says its focus usually is on advertisers and their agencies, though action against an individual endorser can be appropriate in some cases, such as ignoring warnings [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].

What counts as a "material connection"

The FTC's influencer guide describes a material connection as a personal, family or employment relationship, or a financial one, such as a brand paying you or giving you free or discounted products or services [FTC, Disclosures 101]. Some points from the FTC's guidance:

  • Affiliate commissions count. The FTC's answer for affiliate marketers is to disclose the relationship clearly, for example: "I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post" [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].
  • Free products count, even if you weren't asked to post. If a brand gives you free or discounted products and you then mention one, disclose it [FTC, Disclosures 101].
  • "Common knowledge" isn't enough. The FTC rejects the idea that everyone already knows creators earn commissions, because some readers won't [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].
  • No connection, no disclosure. If you bought something yourself and just like it, or you include links purely as a convenience and aren't paid for them, there is no connection to disclose [FTC, Disclosures 101; FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].
  • Your own products. If it's obvious the brand is yours, no disclosure is needed. If it isn't clear, say so [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ]. That's why I simply say "my planner" when I mention something I made myself.

Where a disclosure should go

The FTC's consistent theme is placement: put the disclosure where people will actually see it, close to the recommendation.

  • Near the link or recommendation. "The closer the disclosure is to your recommendation, the better" [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].
  • Not only at the bottom or top. The FTC says readers may miss a disclosure at the very bottom of a page, and may also overlook one at the very top, outside the blog post itself [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].
  • Not only on an About page or profile. Disclosures are likely to be missed there, or anywhere that requires clicking "more" [FTC, Disclosures 101].
  • Not behind a link. A button labeled DISCLOSURE or LEGAL that links elsewhere isn't enough [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].
  • Not one sitewide notice. A single disclosure on your home page won't cover individual posts that readers land on directly [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].
  • In every post that needs it. You can't assume readers saw an earlier post's disclosure [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].

Wording that works, and wording that doesn't

The FTC doesn't require specific wording. It wants clear, simple language in the same language as the endorsement [FTC, Disclosures 101]. Examples from its guidance:

  • Generally fine: "I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post"; "paid link" right next to an affiliate link; "Ad," "#ad," "Sponsored by [brand]"; "Thanks [brand] for the free product" if that's all you got [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].
  • Not enough on its own: "affiliate link," a "buy now" button, "commissionable link," a bare "thank you," "#employee," "#ambassador" without the brand name, "sp," "spon" or "collab" [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ; FTC, Disclosures 101].
  • Don't bury it. Mixing a disclosure into a cluster of hashtags or links makes it easy to miss [FTC, Disclosures 101].

Pins, videos and social posts

Short formats raise their own issues. The FTC notes that tags, likes, pins and similar ways of showing you like a product can be endorsements [FTC, Disclosures 101]. Pinning a picture as part of a sponsored brand campaign "probably requires a disclosure" [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ].

For video, the FTC says the disclosure should be in the video itself, not only in the description, and ideally both spoken and shown on screen. For live streams it should be repeated periodically [FTC, Disclosures 101]. A disclosure in the comments of a post isn't adequate [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ]. And while platform tools for marking paid content are fine to use, the FTC says not to assume they're enough on their own [FTC, Disclosures 101].

Honesty beyond the disclosure

A disclosure doesn't fix a misleading endorsement. According to the FTC [FTC, Disclosures 101; FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ]:

  • You can't talk about your experience with a product you haven't tried.
  • If you've tried it only once, you can't suggest you use it regularly.
  • If you thought it was bad, you can't say it's great.
  • You can't make claims that would need proof the advertiser doesn't have.

The FTC also warns that endorsements showing exceptional results are generally read as typical, and "results may vary" doesn't change that [FTC, Endorsement Guides FAQ]. That's worth remembering for any side-hustle content that features big earnings screenshots.

A simple disclosure checklist

  • Do I get anything of value (commission, free product, payment, perks) from this brand? If yes, disclose.
  • Is the disclosure right next to the link or recommendation, not just at the end or in a footer?
  • Is it in plain words a reader will understand, such as "I earn a commission if you buy through this link"?
  • Is it in this specific post, pin or video, and not only on my About page?
  • For video, is it said and shown in the video itself?
  • Is everything I said about the product true and based on my own experience?

Conclusion

Affiliate income and honest content aren't at odds. The FTC's guidance boils down to a few habits: tell readers about any relationship that could affect how they weigh your recommendation, put that disclosure where they'll see it, use plain words, and only say what's true. If you're unsure about a specific situation, the FTC's own FAQ is detailed and readable, and a qualified attorney can help with the hard cases.
