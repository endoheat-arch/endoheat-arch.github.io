When to Open a Separate Business Bank Account for Your Side Hustle (and What Banks Usually Ask For)

Originally published on Telegraph

  • Why separating money helps bookkeeping and taxes (educational, not advice)

  • FDIC basics for sole proprietors vs other business structures
  • A practical setup checklist

The first customer payment hits your personal checking account. Then a software subscription. Then a refund. Within a month, your everyday account is a mix of groceries, rent, and side-hustle cash, and reconstructing "what the business earned" feels like detective work.

The U.S. Small Business Administration recommends opening a business bank account when you are ready to start accepting or spending money as your business. This post explains that timing in plain English, what documents banks commonly request, why separation makes records clearer for Schedule C–style bookkeeping, and how FDIC deposit insurance treats sole-proprietor accounts. It is general education based on SBA, IRS, and FDIC materials. It is not legal, tax, or financial advice. Banks and states set their own rules; a licensed professional can help with your situation.

When to open one

The SBA's guidance is practical rather than ceremonial: open a business account "when you're ready to start accepting or spending money as your business." It lists common account types (checking, savings, credit card, and merchant services for card payments) and notes that many perks of business banking do not come with a standard personal account (SBA).

For a sole proprietor or early side hustler, that often means the moment you:

  • Start receiving customer payments (Payhip payouts, client invoices, platform deposits)
  • Begin paying business expenses (software, ads, supplies, shipping)
  • Want customers to pay a business name rather than your personal name

You do not need to wait for an LLC. The SBA frames a sole proprietorship as the default when you do business without registering another structure, and still encourages a dedicated business account for that setup (SBA).

Why separation helps bookkeeping and taxes

The IRS does not write a statute that says "you must open a second checking account." What it does emphasize is clear records. In Publication 583, Starting a Business and Keeping Records, the IRS says one of the first things you should do when you start a business is open a business checking account, and that you should keep your business account separate from your personal checking account. It calls the business checkbook a basic source for recording expenses, and advises depositing daily receipts into that account and reconciling it.

An IRS FAQ for sole proprietors who pay personal expenses from a business account is blunt about the bookkeeping upside: "It's a good idea to keep separate business and personal accounts as this makes it easier to keep records" (IRS). Personal, living, or family expenses are generally not deductible as business expenses; mixing accounts makes it harder to keep those lines straight.

Separately, the IRS recordkeeping pages explain that good records help you monitor progress, prepare statements, identify sources of income, track deductible expenses, and support items on your return (IRS). A dedicated account does not replace a ledger or software, but it gives you a cleaner feed of business deposits and withdrawals to reconcile each month.

Documents banks typically ask for

Requirements vary by bank and by how your business is structured. The SBA lists common documents banks ask for when you open a business account (SBA):

  • Employer Identification Number (EIN), or a Social Security number if you are a sole proprietorship
  • Your business's formation documents
  • Ownership agreements
  • Business license

Some banks may ask for more. Expect government-issued photo ID, a verifiable business or home address, and contact details. If you operate under a trade name different from your legal name, banks often want proof of that name (sometimes called a DBA, fictitious name, assumed name, or trade-name filing).

Shop fees and features the way the SBA suggests: introductory offers, interest, transaction fees, early termination fees, and minimum-balance fees all differ by institution (SBA). Confirm the bank is FDIC-insured (or NCUA-insured for a credit union) before you deposit meaningful balances.

DBA and EIN, lightly

DBA. The SBA explains that a "doing business as" name (also called a trade name, fictitious name, or assumed name) may need to be registered with your state, county, or city. Registering a DBA "doesn't provide legal protection by itself," but most states require registration if you use one. The SBA also notes that getting a DBA and a federal tax ID (EIN) can allow you to open a business bank account (SBA).

EIN. An Employer Identification Number is a federal tax ID for a business. The IRS says you can get an EIN for free directly from the IRS, and warns: "Beware of websites that charge for an EIN. You never have to pay a fee for an EIN" (IRS). You generally need an EIN if you have employees, operate as a corporation or partnership, or have certain filing needs; the IRS also notes you can request one for banking or state tax purposes even when you do not need it for federal tax purposes (IRS). The SBA's document list explicitly allows a Social Security number for sole proprietorships when opening an account (SBA). Ask your bank which identifier it accepts for your structure before you apply.

FDIC basics (especially for sole proprietors)

FDIC insurance covers traditional deposit accounts at FDIC-insured banks automatically; you do not apply for it separately (FDIC). Coverage depends on ownership category.

For sole proprietorship (often titled as a DBA) accounts, the FDIC is clear: a sole proprietorship has no separate legal existence for deposit-insurance purposes. The deposit is treated as owned by the sole proprietor and insured as that person's single account, added together with any other single accounts the owner has at the same bank, up to $250,000 in total (FDIC). Opening a "business" labeled sole-prop account at the same bank as your personal checking does not create a second $250,000 bucket by itself.

By contrast, deposits owned by a validly formed corporation, partnership, or unincorporated association engaged in an independent activity are generally insured up to $250,000 for that entity at the same bank, separately from the owners' personal accounts (FDIC). The FDIC's 2026 consumer note on small-business finances restates the same sole-prop vs entity distinction (FDIC).

If balances may approach the limit, use the FDIC's EDIE estimator or call 1-877-ASK-FDIC. This is insurance education, not a recommendation of where to bank.

Practical setup checklist

1. Decide you are accepting or spending money as a business (SBA timing). 2. Confirm whether you will use your legal name or a DBA; register a DBA if your locality requires it for the name you use (SBA). 3. Decide whether you will apply with an SSN (sole prop, if the bank allows) or get a free EIN from IRS.gov (IRS). 4. Gather ID, address, EIN or SSN, and any license or formation papers the bank lists (SBA). 5. Compare fees, minimum balances, and digital tools across a few FDIC- or NCUA-insured institutions. 6. Open the account; route platform payouts and client payments there. 7. Deposit business receipts into the business account and keep personal spending on the personal side (IRS Pub. 583). 8. Reconcile monthly; keep statements and receipts with your tax records (IRS). 9. If you are a sole proprietor at the same bank as your personal accounts, remember FDIC single-account aggregation (FDIC). 10. Optional: use a simple monthly planner such as Calm Money Monthly Planner so the new account feeds a habit, not a pile of uncategorized exports.

The bottom line

A separate business checking account is one of the simplest operational upgrades a side hustle can make. The SBA ties the decision to the moment you accept or spend money as a business; the IRS treats a dedicated business checkbook as foundational recordkeeping; and the FDIC explains that sole-prop "business" deposits usually share the owner's $250,000 single-account coverage at that bank. Gather the documents your bank asks for, keep business money on one side of the ledger, and reconcile on a schedule you will actually keep.
