Copyright Basics for Printable and Planner Sellers: What You Own, What You Can't Protect, and What to Check Before You Sell

If you sell printables, planners or other digital downloads, copyright touches almost every file you upload. It decides what you actually own, what a competitor can legally copy, and what you need permission to use. The good news is that the U.S. Copyright Office publishes plain-language guidance on most of the questions small sellers ask.

This post is an explainer based on U.S. Copyright Office pages and Payhip's own terms. It covers U.S. law only, and it is general education, not legal advice. For a specific situation, the Copyright Office itself suggests consulting an attorney.

What copyright covers, and when it starts

Copyright protects original works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium of expression, including literary and artistic works, and it covers both published and unpublished work. For a seller, that can include the written guidance inside a planner, original illustrations, photos and cover artwork.

Protection is automatic. Your work is protected the moment it is created and fixed in a tangible form, and saving a file counts, since the Office notes a work can be perceptible "with the aid of a machine or device." You do not need to publish it first, either: publication is not necessary for copyright protection.

For works created after January 1, 1978, protection generally lasts the life of the author plus 70 years. If you sell under a pen name, note that for a pseudonymous work the term is 95 years from first publication or 120 years from creation, whichever expires first.

One popular myth to drop: mailing a copy to yourself, the so-called "poor man's copyright," has no provision in the copyright law and is not a substitute for registration.

What copyright does not protect

This is the section that surprises most planner sellers. The Copyright Office's Works Not Protected by Copyright (Circular 33) says several things common in printables are not protected on their own:

  • Blank forms. Forms "designed for recording information" that do not themselves convey information are uncopyrightable. The Office's examples include time cards, account books, diaries, address books, order forms, and date books and schedulers (Circular 33).
  • Layout and format. The general layout of a page, book cover, poster or form is treated as "a template for expression" and is not registrable (Circular 33).
  • Typefaces and lettering. The Office says copyright does not protect typeface or mere variations of typographic ornamentation or lettering (Circular 33).
  • Names, titles and short phrases. Product names, titles and slogans are uncopyrightable, though some may be protected as trademarks.
  • Ideas, systems and methods. Copyright does not protect ideas, concepts, systems, or methods of doing something, only the way you express them.

That cuts both ways. A plain budget grid or weekly schedule is hard to claim as yours alone, and other sellers can make similar grids. What can be protected is the creative material you add. Circular 33 explains that a blank form may incorporate images or text that is sufficiently creative, and in that case protection covers only that original text or artwork, not the form itself. Original illustrations, written prompts and instructions, and a distinctive cover design are where your own authorship lives.

Using other people's work

The default assumption should be that a work is protected. The Office notes that since any original work fixed in a tangible medium, including a computer file, is protected on creation, most works may be assumed to be protected unless there is clear information otherwise.

Changing something does not make it yours. Only the copyright owner can prepare or authorize a new version, so you cannot claim copyright to another's work, no matter how much you change it, without the owner's consent.

Fair use exists, but it is not a shortcut for products. Courts weigh four factors: the purpose and character of the use (including whether it is commercial), the nature of the work, how much is used, and the effect on the market for the original. The Office stresses there is no formula and no safe number of words, lines or pages. In cases of doubt, the Copyright Office recommends that permission be obtained.

For clip art, fonts, photos and templates you buy, the practical step is to read the license that came with them and confirm it allows commercial use in products you resell.

AI-generated art and text

Many sellers now use AI tools for illustrations or copy. In Part 2 of its report on copyright and AI, published January 29, 2025, the Copyright Office concluded that AI outputs can be protected only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements. That can include creative arrangements or modifications of the output, but not the mere provision of prompts. The Office also confirmed that using AI to assist, or including AI material in a larger human-made work, does not bar copyrightability of the human contribution.

In practice, the parts of a product you write, arrange or meaningfully edit yourself are the parts you have the strongest claim to.

Registration: optional, but it changes your options

Registration is voluntary, but you have to register before bringing an infringement lawsuit over a U.S. work in federal court. Registered works may also be eligible for statutory damages and attorney's fees in successful litigation.

The Office's fee schedule lists $45 for an electronic filing covering one work by a single author who is also the claimant and not made for hire, and $65 for the Standard Application.

For smaller disputes there is the Copyright Claims Board, a voluntary alternative to federal court for claims up to $30,000 in total damages. You need at least a submitted registration application to file there, you don't need an attorney, and the other side can opt out.

What platforms like Payhip expect

Payhip's Terms of Use say you keep the rights in your content and grant Payhip a license to distribute copies to buyers. You also warrant that your content does not infringe anyone else's intellectual property, and Payhip says it may remove a file it believes infringes without prior notice. The same terms state that the buyer license is for personal, non-commercial use and prohibits reproduction or distribution.

A pre-launch checklist

  • List every element in the file that you did not create yourself: fonts, clip art, photos, quotes, templates.
  • For each one, confirm you have a license that covers commercial resale, and keep a copy of it.
  • Avoid characters, logos and brand names you don't own; titles and slogans can raise trademark issues even when copyright doesn't apply.
  • Note which parts are your original authorship (illustrations, written guidance, cover art).
  • If you used AI, note what you added or changed by hand.
  • Decide whether registering your most important works is worth the filing fee.
  • Keep a simple record of what you created and when. I track product records alongside my numbers in the Calm Money Monthly Planner, but any spreadsheet works.

Conclusion

Copyright starts automatically, but it protects your creative expression, not a blank grid, a layout, a font or a product name. Build products around original artwork and writing, license everything else properly, and treat fair use as a narrow legal defense rather than a plan. When real money or a real dispute is involved, talk to a qualified attorney.
