How to Use a Teacher Lesson Planner Printable to Plan Your Week in One Sitting (Lesson Plans, Grade Book and Attendance)

Originally published on Telegraph

It is Sunday night. Your lesson ideas live in a slide deck, a sticky note on the laptop, two browser tabs and the back of a staff meeting agenda. Grades are half in your head. You promised a parent a call back on Thursday, and you are not sure it happened. Below is a simple paper system that works for classroom teachers, specialists, tutors and homeschool parents. It covers a Sunday planning routine, how to fill a weekly grid without rewriting everything, and how to keep grades, attendance and parent contacts tidy in one binder.

This guide is general organizing help only. It is not curriculum, instructional or education policy advice. Always follow your school's, district's or state's requirements for lesson plans, grading, attendance and record keeping, and check with your administrator when you are unsure what has to be submitted or kept digitally.

1. Set up your teacher lesson planner printable in a binder once

Spend about 30 minutes setting up the binder before the next planning day. After that, each week is just filling in pages.

  • A 1- or 1.5-inch three-ring binder (or a sturdy clipboard if you prefer to carry only this week)
  • Tab dividers: Info, Year, Weekly Plans, Lesson Details, Grades, Attendance, Parent Contacts
  • A pencil pouch with a pencil, a fine-tip pen and two or three highlighters
  • A few sheet protectors for the pages you look at every day

Put your teacher info page in the front. Fill in your schedule, room number, duties, planning period and anything you get asked about constantly, such as the bell schedule or the copy room code. When a substitute or co-teacher needs the basics, this page answers most of it.

2. Map the year (roughly) before you plan any week

A year-at-a-glance page keeps weekly plans pointed in the right direction. You do not need every detail, just the shape of the year.

  • Mark breaks, testing windows, report card dates and school events you already know
  • Block out units or themes in pencil across the months
  • Leave a buffer week or two before big breaks for review and catch-up

Pencil matters here. Calendars change, and erasing beats rewriting the whole page. When you sit down each Sunday, a glance at this map tells you where the week fits in the bigger plan.

3. Use a weekly lesson plan template, not a blank page

The biggest time saver is a weekly lesson plan template with the same layout every week. Your brain stops deciding where things go and starts deciding what to teach.

A good weekly grid has five days across the top and your periods, subjects or class blocks down the side. In each box, write only what you need to teach from it:

  • The topic or lesson title
  • One line for the main activity
  • Page numbers, links to look up later or materials to pull
  • A small symbol for things to copy or prep (a circled "C" works)

Keep it brief. The weekly grid is a map, not a script. If a lesson needs more detail, that is what the detailed lesson template is for.

Tip: Color-code by subject or class period with highlighters. When you open the binder on a busy Tuesday, you can find "3rd period" at a glance.

4. Save the detailed lesson template for lessons that need it

Not every lesson needs a full write-up. Use the detailed lesson template for new units, observed lessons, lessons a substitute will teach, or anything you want to reuse next year. It walks through:

  • Objectives (what students should know or be able to do)
  • Standards (whatever your school asks you to list)
  • Materials and prep
  • Warm-up
  • Guided practice
  • Assessment (how you will check understanding)
  • Differentiation (supports and extensions)

File finished templates behind the Lesson Details tab. Next year, you can pull a page, adjust it and save yourself an evening.

5. A 45-minute Sunday planning routine

Here is a simple routine you can adapt. Set a timer if it helps you stop when you are done.

  • Minutes 0–5: Look at the year-at-a-glance page and note anything special this week (assemblies, early release, testing, holidays).
  • Minutes 5–25: Fill the weekly lesson plan grid, starting with the class or subject that takes the most prep.
  • Minutes 25–35: Write a detailed lesson template for any lesson that needs one.
  • Minutes 35–40: Make a short prep list: copies, materials, links to queue up.
  • Minutes 40–45: Check the parent contact log for any follow-ups due this week and add them to your grid.

If 45 minutes feels like a lot, start with just the weekly grid. Even a half-filled grid makes Monday morning calmer than an empty one.

6. Keep a printable grade book as your paper backup

Many schools require grades in an online system, and that system is the official record. A printable grade book is still useful as a quick working copy, a backup when the Wi-Fi is down, or the main record for tutors and homeschool families.

  • Write student names once at the start of the term (print a fresh page each grading period)
  • Label each of the 8 assignment columns with a short name and the date
  • Use a consistent mark for missing work, such as an empty circle you fill in when it comes in
  • Transfer grades to your school's system on a set day each week so the two never drift far apart

That last habit is the important one. One "grade entry day" a week keeps the paper and online books in sync.

7. Track attendance on a simple classroom attendance sheet

A 20-day attendance sheet covers about a month of school days per page. Even if you take official attendance online, a paper attendance sheet in the binder helps you spot patterns quickly, such as a student who is always tardy on Mondays, and gives a substitute something easy to use.

Use simple codes and write them at the top of the page: P for present, A for absent, T for tardy, E for excused (or whatever codes your school uses). Start a new page each month and file the old ones behind the tab.

8. Log every parent contact the same day

A parent contact log protects your time and your memory. When you call, email or meet with a family, take one minute to note:

  • Date and student name
  • How you contacted them (call, email, note home, meeting)
  • What you talked about, in a sentence or two
  • Any follow-up and the date it is due

When a question comes up weeks later, you have the details on one page instead of searching through your inbox. Add follow-ups to your weekly grid on Sunday so they do not slip.

9. A homeschool lesson planner printable works the same way

Homeschool parents can use the same pages with small changes. Use the class periods on the weekly grid as subjects or children instead. Use the grade book for completed work or scores, the attendance sheet to record school days if you choose to keep track, and the parent contact log for co-op teachers, tutors or outside classes. Check your own state's homeschool rules for any records you are expected to keep. This guide cannot tell you what those are.
