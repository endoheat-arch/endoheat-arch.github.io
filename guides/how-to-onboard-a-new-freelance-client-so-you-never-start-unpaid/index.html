How to Onboard a New Freelance Client (So You Never Start Unpaid)

Originally published on Telegraph

The client said yes. That's the fun part. Then the first week turns into chasing a logo file, waiting on a contract, and guessing who actually approves the work. A simple onboarding routine fixes most of that, and you only have to build it once.

Why onboarding is the real product

Most freelancers sell the deliverable and treat the start of the project as a handshake. Clients remember the chaos of week one more than the polish of week six. When money, scope, and access are fuzzy, you eat the cost: unpaid hours, scope creep, and stalled feedback. A repeatable onboarding sequence turns "yes" into a clear working relationship before you open a blank file.

You do not need a law firm or a fancy CRM for this. You need a checklist you reuse, a few templates you edit, and the habit of refusing to start until the deposit and agreement are done.

1. Get the money and paperwork done before any work

Send the proposal or quote, get the agreement signed, and collect the deposit before you open a design file or write a word. Write down the payment schedule (for example 50% up front, 50% on delivery), the invoice due terms, and any tax forms the client needs from you.

If you start before the deposit lands, you're doing free work and hoping. That hope rarely pays. Put the rule in your welcome email so it does not feel personal later: work begins after the agreement is signed and the first payment clears.

Keep a simple money checklist: proposal sent, agreement signed, deposit received, invoice numbered, tax form exchanged if needed. Check each box before you schedule kickoff.

2. Write a one-page project brief together

Ask the client to answer a few plain questions: What outcome do you want? What must be included? What is out of scope? How will we know it worked? Who is the end user? Keep the answers on one page.

When a "quick extra" request shows up later, the out-of-scope line is your polite, written answer. You are not being difficult; you are protecting the timeline you both agreed to. If the extra is worth doing, you quote it as a change order with a new price and date.

3. Lock deliverables, quantities, and dates

"A new website" is not a deliverable. "Five pages, two rounds of revisions, delivered as a live site by Nov 20" is. List each deliverable with its format, quantity, number of revision rounds, and due date. Share it and get a simple "looks good" reply in writing.

Vague deliverables create endless revisions. Specific ones create clear finish lines. If the client wants something open-ended ("we'll know it when we see it"), translate that into measurable checkpoints: draft, review, revisions, final. Put those dates on a one-page timeline so both sides can see the path.

4. Collect assets and access in one request

Send a single checklist instead of ten separate emails: brand files, fonts, photos, copy, logins, hosting or repo access, and who holds each one. Ask the client to share passwords through a password manager or a secure share link, not plain email.

Missing assets are the quiet killer of freelance timelines. One consolidated request on day one beats a week of "just one more file." Mark each item as received or still waiting, and do not start production work that depends on missing pieces.

5. Name the decision-maker

Find out who gives feedback, who approves, and who just needs to stay informed. Two people giving conflicting notes is one of the most common ways freelance projects drag. One named approver saves weeks.

Write the names on a contacts sheet: day-to-day contact, final approver, billing contact, and technical contact if there is one. Ask how fast feedback will usually come (for example, three business days). When feedback is late, you can point to the agreed window instead of arguing.

6. Run a short kickoff call and follow up the same day

Use a short agenda: goals, timeline, milestones, communication channel, and next steps for each side. Keep the call under 30 minutes when you can. Afterward, send a recap email with the dates and who owes what.

Check in again at the end of week one so small problems don't become big ones. A five-line status note ("here's done, here's blocked, here's what I need from you") beats a surprise at the deadline.

7. Reuse your words

You'll write the same welcome email, scope note, and follow-up for every client. Save good versions as templates, or use an AI assistant to draft them faster, then edit so they sound like you and stay accurate. Never send AI-written client emails without reading them first, and keep client data private.

Templates are not laziness. They are how you stay consistent when you are tired or juggling three projects. The AI Prompt Pack is built for that drafting step; the onboarding pack is built for the paper trail.

A first-week checklist you can copy

  • Agreement signed and deposit received
  • One-page brief filled and confirmed
  • Deliverables, revision rounds, and dates listed
  • Assets and access checklist sent and tracked
  • Approver and contacts named
  • Kickoff held; recap emailed the same day
  • End-of-week-one check-in scheduled

If any box is empty, pause production work that depends on it. That pause is cheaper than rework.
